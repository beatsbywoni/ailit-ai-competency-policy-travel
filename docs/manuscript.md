# When standards travel: National vernacularisation of international AI competency frameworks across 25 education systems

## Abstract

How international standards travel into, and are reshaped by, national education systems is a long-standing question in comparative education. This article applies it to the AI competency frameworks issued by UNESCO (2024) and the OECD–European Commission (final version, June 2026), aligning 25 countries' AI-in-education policy texts (67 documents, eight languages) computationally with the frameworks' verbatim definitional sentences. Two findings emerge. First, across the three frameworks operative at harvest, 21 countries adopt a canonical problem-scoping and managing-AI register while Korea and Ireland alone foreground application-skills and AI-pedagogy vocabulary, carried by 2022 documents that predate the frameworks. Second, the OECD–EC draft-to-final revision reconceptualised its fourth domain and dissolved that axis: the same frozen texts classify differently before and after revision, while the Korea–Ireland finding, established by the two UNESCO frameworks, is unaffected. Standards travel not only into national systems but through their own revision, with implications for framework designers and PISA 2029.

**Keywords:** AI in education policy; computational policy analysis; cross-national policy analysis; diffusion of innovations; European Union; policy borrowing; policy vernacularisation

## Introduction

Within a twenty-one-month window beginning in September 2024, the world's two principal multilateral education-policy organisations issued three AI competency frameworks for school education: UNESCO's *AI Competency Framework for Students* (Miao and Shiohira 2024) and *AI Competency Framework for Teachers* (Miao and Cukurova 2024), and the OECD–European Commission's *Empowering Learners for the Age of AI* — released as a review draft in May 2025 and in final form on 18 June 2026 (OECD and European Commission 2026). Each framework offers a structured taxonomy of the knowledge, skills, attitudes, and ethical orientations that learners and teachers are expected to develop, and each is intended as a benchmark against which national curricula can be reviewed. The OECD–EC framework carries two further institutional weights of particular relevance to a European readership: it is designed to inform the innovative domain of the PISA 2029 Media and AI Literacy assessment, and it aligns explicitly with the AI-literacy obligation that Article 4 of the EU Artificial Intelligence Act (European Parliament and Council 2024) has placed on deployers of AI systems since February 2025. AI literacy in schools has, in short, become a live question of European soft governance rather than only a curricular aspiration.

These releases coincide with an unusual concentration of national policy activity on AI in education (Holmes and Tuomi 2022; Crompton and Burke 2023) and create the conditions for a question that comparative-education scholarship has long pursued in other policy domains (Ball 2003; Phillips and Ochs 2003; Steiner-Khamsi 2014; Verger, Steiner-Khamsi, and Lubienski 2017): how does an international policy standard, once issued by a supranational organisation, travel into national education systems, and through what mechanisms is it reshaped, indigenised, or selectively translated? Anderson-Levitt and Gardinier (2021), introducing a special issue on global flows of competency-based education, propose that the productive question concerns the *polysemy, hybridity, and silences* a standard acquires as it crosses contexts. The closest prior cross-national study of national AI policy (Schiff 2022) found the educational dimension largely absent from national AI *strategies*; the post-2024 wave produces, for the first time, a corpus in which the educational dimension is the explicit subject.

This article addresses that question through an analysis of 25 countries' AI-in-education policy texts harvested between 28 and 30 May 2026 and frozen at that date: 67 documents spanning eight languages, including national strategies, ministry curricular guidance, teacher-professional-development plans, and supplementary curricula. Every sentence is matched computationally against the verbatim definitional sentences of the frameworks. The resulting country-level adherence patterns are grouped using hierarchical clustering, tested against a six-module pre-specified robustness battery, and replicated against each framework in turn — a cross-framework design that distinguishes findings that hold regardless of anchor system from artefacts of any single one. Because the OECD–EC framework was finalised after the corpus was frozen, the draft-to-final revision supplies an unplanned but instructive natural experiment: the same national texts, aligned to the same framework before and after its authors revised it.

Four research questions structure the analysis. **RQ1** When national education systems take up the vocabulary of international AI competency frameworks, do their policy texts converge on a shared conceptual register, or do they diverge, and if so on which conceptual axes? **RQ2** Is any divergence pattern *framework-invariant* — does it persist when the analysis is re-run against an anchor system independently authored by a different organisation, and against the same framework after revision? **RQ3** Does the assigned group of any country survive a six-module robustness battery? **RQ4** Read through policy-borrowing stages (Phillips and Ochs 2003) and diffusion-of-innovation typology (Rogers 2003), are the countries that diverge from the canonical register *imitative* adopters, *indigenisation* actors, or *frontier* adopters whose own prior framing the international standard subsequently makes legible?

The article makes three contributions. *Empirically*, it documents a stable two-group partition — Korea and Ireland deviating, 21 countries canonical — across the three anchor systems operative at harvest, with both deviating signatures carried by 2022 documents that predate the frameworks. *Methodologically*, it shows that the cross-framework replication design functions as a measurement-invariance check, and that the same check applied across a framework's own revision reveals how much of a "national" classification is in fact a property of the anchor vocabulary. *Theoretically*, it reads the canonical mass as DiMaggio and Powell's (1983) *mimetic isomorphism* operating through European soft-governance instruments, the deviating pair as Rogers's (2003) *innovator/early-adopter* category, and the temporal direction of their signature as Steiner-Khamsi's (2014) *borrowing-as-legitimation*.

## Background

### AI literacy as a structured construct

The construct of AI literacy has matured rapidly since Long and Magerko (2020) offered a foundational taxonomy. Ng et al. (2021), reviewing thirty studies, distilled it into a four-aspect schema that anticipates the four-aspect structure of UNESCO's Student Framework (Miao and Shiohira 2024); Touretzky et al. (2019) cast the same construct as the *Five Big Ideas in AI for K-12*. On the teacher side, Mikeladze, Meijer, and Verhoeff's (2024) critical review of AI competence frameworks for educators identifies five framework types and documents both the proliferation of frameworks and their convergence on a small set of competence domains — the condition under which UNESCO's teacher framework (Miao and Cukurova 2024) could be issued as a consolidating reference. Empirical work has expanded rapidly (Casal-Otero et al. 2023; Chiu 2023; Kong, Cheung, and Zhang 2021; Su, Ng, and Chu 2023; Yim and Su 2024). The definitional stability that Mikeladze et al. (2024) and Ng et al. (2021) document is the measurement premise of the present analysis: treating the UNESCO and OECD–EC anchors as a stable instrument rather than as one more contestable framework.

### Three frameworks, three concept spaces

The three anchor frameworks address overlapping conceptual territory but partition it differently. The UNESCO Student Framework's 12 anchors are organised in a four-aspect by three-level matrix (human-centred mindset; ethics of AI; AI techniques and applications; AI system design). The UNESCO Teacher Framework's 5 anchors collapse the four aspects into five teacher-facing macro-categories, including *AI Pedagogy* and *AI for Professional Development*. The OECD–EC framework reorganises the construct into four learner-activity domains. In the May 2025 review draft these were *Engaging with*, *Creating with*, *Managing*, and *Designing AI*; in the June 2026 final version the fourth domain was renamed *Shape AI* and — as the results below show — substantively reconceptualised from hands-on construction towards critical-reflective agency. Because the two UNESCO frameworks share an institutional author, the cross-framework design tests two-organisation rather than three-organisation independence; because the OECD–EC framework exists in two versions, it additionally tests within-framework invariance across revision.

### Policy borrowing, diffusion, and isomorphism

National responses to international policy initiatives are theorised under three complementary lenses, each generating a distinct prediction. *Comparative-education policy borrowing* (Ball 2003; Phillips and Ochs 2003; Steiner-Khamsi 2014; Verger, Steiner-Khamsi, and Lubienski 2017) treats borrowing as a four-stage process — attraction, decision, implementation, internalisation — and emphasises that borrowing often *legitimates* prior domestic policy through reference to an external standard rather than importing substantive content. Anderson-Levitt and Gardinier (2021) refine this by directing attention to the polysemy, hybridity, and silences a global standard acquires as it travels; Buckner (2019) demonstrates the same pattern for higher-education internationalisation, showing that a single global model yields systematically different *national interpretations*. The lens predicts that the most legible cross-national variation will lie not in whether countries adhere to framework concepts but in *which* sub-aspects are foregrounded, and that countries whose framing predates the framework should exhibit the most distinctive signatures. The *diffusion-of-innovation* lens (Rogers 2003) generates a three-tier prediction: frontier adopters whose policy text predates or coincides with framework publication; fast followers whose text post-dates it; and recipients whose adoption is nominal. The *institutional isomorphism* lens (DiMaggio and Powell 1983) predicts that the dominant mechanism for the early majority is *mimetic* — under uncertainty about what an AI-in-education policy should contain, ministries converge on the high-status framework exemplar.

Read together, the lenses generate a falsifiable prediction structure. If mimetic isomorphism dominates, the largest cluster should foreground the most legible "framework-correct" register. If borrowing-as-legitimation applies, the small set of countries whose text predates the framework should occupy a distinct deviating cluster on the sub-aspects the framework subsequently makes legible. If the Rogers innovator category is operative, those same countries should be identifiable independently as early movers. A pre-specified hypothesis from a five-country pilot anticipated four adoption signatures resolving into four roughly equal clusters; the 25-country results do not support this, and the revision to a one-canonical-plus-one-deviation structure is itself a finding.

### Competence frameworks as European soft-governance instruments

For a European readership a fourth lens is indispensable, because the OECD–EC framework does not arrive on a blank slate. It inherits a specifically European template of competence-framework governance: the *DigComp* framework for citizens (now DigComp 2.2; Vuorikari, Kluzer, and Punie 2022) and the *DigCompEdu* framework for educators (Redecker 2017) established, over a decade, the form of a reference document that national systems are invited to self-assess against rather than obliged to adopt — soft law operating through comparability, reference tools such as SELFIE, and the ordinary machinery of what Grek (2009) called *governing by numbers*. The OECD–EC framework's front matter locates it in exactly this lineage, drawing on AI4K12, on UNESCO, and on DigComp/DigCompEdu, and positioning it as the reference point for PISA 2029 and for national implementation of the AI Act's literacy obligation. Two implications follow for the present analysis. First, the mimetic isomorphism predicted above has a concrete European mechanism: a competence framework whose reference status is secured by its ties to a comparative assessment (PISA) and to a binding regulation (the AI Act) is precisely the kind of high-status exemplar that uncertain ministries converge on. Second, the framework is itself a vernacularisation of prior frameworks — standards travel *through* standards — and its draft-to-final revision is a further moment of travel that the present design can observe directly.

## Materials and Methods

This section describes the corpus, anchor sentences, sentence-matching procedure, clustering, and robustness battery at a level that allows readers without a computational background to evaluate the inferences that follow; each procedure is introduced in ordinary language before any technical term. Full specifications, including the self-contained validation of the alignment threshold and the algorithmic details of clustering and the robustness modules, are in the Supporting Information (appendix E).

### Corpus

Sixty-eight national AI-in-education policy documents were harvested between 28 and 30 May 2026 from 25 countries selected to span four OECD regional groupings, a range of GDP-per-capita quartiles, and both pre- and post-September 2024 publication dates. One Chinese-language scanned PDF was dropped before analysis, leaving 67 documents (36,098 sentences after segmentation) across eight languages. The corpus was **frozen at the harvest date**; no documents have been added or removed since. This freeze is analytically consequential: the OECD–EC framework was finalised on 18 June 2026, so none of the national texts could have been written to its final wording. Genres include national AI strategies, ministry curriculum guidance, AI-in-schools frameworks, parliamentary research briefs, and one supranational coordination document. Per-document provenance is in appendix A.

### Anchor sentences

Three framework documents supplied the anchor sentences — the verbatim definitional paragraph of each sub-principle — against which corpus sentences were matched: UNESCO Student (12 anchors; Miao and Shiohira 2024), UNESCO Teacher (5; Miao and Cukurova 2024), and OECD–EC (4). For the OECD–EC framework two anchor sets were used. The *draft* anchors, extracted from the May 2025 review draft that was the operative document at harvest, are the pre-specified third anchor system. The *final* anchors, extracted from the domain-opener paragraphs of the June 2026 published framework (pp. 26, 32, 36, 40), were added after the corpus was frozen and are reported as a post-hoc within-framework revision test; because the sentence encoder reads at most 128 tokens, each final anchor is the longest run of initial sentences of its paragraph that the encoder can read in full (three or four sentences), a rule applied mechanically and documented, with the complete paragraphs, in appendix B. Three of the twelve student anchors were rewritten using only material from the same framework section after a pre-specified cohesion check (appendix B).

### Sentence matching and adherence

Each sentence in the corpus was compared with each anchor sentence for similarity of meaning, using a multilingual model that represents sentences in eight languages in a common space (`paraphrase-multilingual-mpnet-base-v2`; Reimers and Gurevych 2019). A sentence was assigned to its closest anchor when the similarity score exceeded 0.35; otherwise it was treated as unaligned. This supervised-anchor procedure is the natural complement to the unsupervised topic-modelling approach that Eğin, Onan, and Yildiz Durak (2025) apply to AI-in-education text in this journal: where topic modelling discovers themes, anchor alignment measures adherence to themes fixed in advance by the frameworks themselves. The overall alignment rate under the student anchors was 66.8%.

The 0.35 threshold is validated within the present study rather than inherited. Two checks establish that the substantive finding does not depend on it. First, re-running the full clustering at thresholds of 0.35, 0.40, 0.45, and 0.50 returns the same two-group partition with Korea and Ireland as the deviating pair at every value; only at 0.55, where the alignment rate falls to 21.5% and the adherence rows become too sparse, does the partition degrade (appendix E, table E1). Second, the discriminant-validity module reported below shows that at 0.35 the anchors fire on 0.0% of off-domain text, so the threshold is not so permissive as to admit non-AI prose. The 0.35 value therefore sits at the permissive end of a 15-point range over which the finding is invariant, and is retained as the pre-specified value. For each country, the share of aligned sentences assigned to each anchor produces a *country adherence row*, the unit of analysis; the approach sits within the *text as data* tradition (Grimmer and Stewart 2013; Wilkerson and Casas 2017; Gentzkow, Kelly, and Taddy 2019; Rodriguez and Spirling 2022) and extends Butterfuss and Doran's (2025) use of embeddings for standards alignment from within-jurisdiction to cross-national analysis.

### Grouping by adherence pattern

Each country's adherence row is a distribution of its policy attention across anchors. Countries were grouped by how similar these distributions are, using a standard distance measure for probability distributions (Hellinger distance; Cha 2007) and agglomerative hierarchical clustering (Everitt et al. 2011) with Ward linkage (Ward 1963; Murtagh and Legendre 2014). The number of groups was chosen by the silhouette criterion (Rousseeuw 1987) over two to six groups. Countries with fewer than 50 aligned sentences were excluded from clustering because distances computed on so few observations are dominated by noise; Australia (32–42 aligned sentences) and the United Arab Emirates (5–7) are reported with a small-corpus flag throughout.

### Robustness battery and cross-framework replication

Six pre-specified checks test whether the grouping is an artefact of something other than policy content: (1) inverse weighting (each document counts equally, removing single-document dominance); (2) leave-one-out (each country's largest document dropped); (3) bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (1,000 resamples); (4) uniform-cap sub-sampling at 500 sentences per country (1,000 resamples); (5) a temporal split at September 2024; (6) discriminant validity against off-domain text and against corporate AI-ethics text. Specifications are in appendix E and full results in appendix C. The pipeline is re-run against each anchor system in turn, and a country-level *replication score* counts the pre-specified frameworks (of three) under which a country falls in the deviating group.

### Use of AI tools

The author used Anthropic's Claude (Opus 4 and successor releases) during the project for three purposes: organising the literature search, refactoring the analysis code, and copy-editing draft prose. It was not used to generate or interpret data, to select anchors, to specify thresholds or clustering settings, or to draw conclusions; all analytic decisions and all interpretation are the author's, and every AI-assisted passage was reviewed and edited before inclusion. The pipeline itself uses a sentence-embedding model as a measurement instrument, as described above; this is the study's method, not an authoring aid.

### Reproducibility

The pipeline is implemented as shell-orchestrated Python scripts (versions in appendix E) in the replication archive linked in the Data Availability Statement. Every step is idempotent and a single command reproduces the principal analysis; the OECD–EC final-anchor re-run is a separate, additive script that leaves the pre-specified outputs untouched.

## Results

### Cross-framework cluster identity

The headline finding is that national AI-in-education policy texts fall into two groups, and that two countries — Korea and Ireland — sit in the smaller group under every anchor system that was operative when the corpus was harvested (table 1; fig. 1). Under each of the three pre-specified frameworks, clustering yields a clear two-group partition (silhouette 0.379 for the 12-anchor student framework, 0.473 for the 5-anchor teacher framework, 0.483 for the 4-anchor OECD–EC draft; appendix D). The larger, *canonical* group (17–21 countries depending on the framework) is characterised by adherence to *AI System Design* and *Managing AI* concepts. The smaller, *deviating* group (2–6 countries) is characterised by *Application Skills* under the student framework, *AI Pedagogy* under the teacher framework, and *Designing AI* under the OECD–EC draft. The United States joins the deviating group under the teacher and draft-OECD frameworks but not the student framework (replication score 2); Finland, Canada, and Sweden join only under the draft-OECD framework (score 1); the remaining 18 eligible countries score 0. Framework-pair agreement is 95.5% between the two UNESCO frameworks, 82.6% between UNESCO student and OECD–EC draft, and 86.4% between UNESCO teacher and OECD–EC draft.

The deviating signal attaches to a different macro-concept under each framework — students *using* AI tools, teachers *integrating* AI, learners *designing* AI systems — and the deviating set expands monotonically as anchor dimensionality falls (2 → 3 → 6 countries). The minimal-overlap set, Korea and Ireland, is the framework-invariant finding; the expanded sets are framework-specific findings about distinct kinds of pedagogy-of-AI deviation.

### Framework revision as a within-framework invariance test

The OECD–EC framework's revision between May 2025 and June 2026 offers a test that the pre-specified design could not have planned: the same 25 frozen national corpora aligned to the same framework before and after its authors revised it. The result is that the discriminating axis dissolves (table 3). Under the draft anchors the fourth domain, *Designing AI*, was the axis on which countries differed most (cross-country standard deviation 14.4 percentage points; range 3.8–69.0). Under the final anchors the renamed fourth domain, *Shape AI*, captures little of any country's policy text (mean 6.4%, standard deviation 5.1, range 1.0–25.3), and the attention that had loaded on it migrates to *Manage AI* (mean 31.6% → 45.6%) and *Create with AI* (14.7% → 22.8%). Between-country dispersion of whole profiles, measured as the mean pairwise Hellinger distance, falls from 0.186 to 0.144, a reduction of 23%. Korea's fourth-domain share drops from 43.4% to 9.5%, within one standard deviation of the cross-country mean, and its distance from the canonical centroid halves (0.246 → 0.122). Ireland retains the highest *Shape AI* share in the corpus (25.3%, against 10.6% for the United States, 10.4% for France, 10.1% for Norway, and under 10% for every other country) and remains the country furthest from the canonical centroid (0.383 → 0.227), but the residual spread is insufficient for a two-group solution: the pre-specified two-cluster cut isolates Estonia (n = 54 aligned sentences) as a singleton and places all other 22 eligible countries in one cluster. Excluding Estonia, or raising the eligibility floor to 100 aligned sentences, does not recover a Korea–Ireland group at any number of clusters under either linkage; the only minority structure that emerges is Ireland on its own (appendix E.7a).

The substantive reason is visible in the anchor text itself. The draft's *Designing AI* defined the domain through hands-on exploration of "how data, design choices, and model behavior influence" AI systems — vocabulary that national tool-use text readily matches. The final *Shape AI* redefines it as examining "the relationship between the technical underpinnings of AI systems and the human choices that influence their behaviour" and seeing "AI systems as deliberate and human-driven, rather than inevitable" — a critical-reflective orientation that national policy text written before June 2026 barely touches. The framework's authors moved the constructive, tool-use content of the fourth domain into the second and third domains and gave the fourth a new meaning. Against the *final* OECD–EC vocabulary, therefore, Korea reads as canonical, and the cross-framework replication of the Korea–Ireland pair holds for the three anchor systems operative at harvest but not for the revised fourth. This is reported as a finding, not as a failure of replication: it is the design working as intended, and its interpretation is taken up in the Discussion.

### Aggregated macro-aspect profiles

The two-group partition is not an artefact of a single anchor. Aggregating each country's 12 student-framework anchors to the four UNESCO macro-aspects (table 2; fig. 3) shows that the deviating pair differs from the canonical mass on one axis: *AI techniques and applications* (Korea–Ireland centroid 36.7% against a canonical centroid of 13.8%) versus *AI system design* (25.7% against 41.4%). Human-centred-mindset shares are virtually identical across groups (27.9% versus 27.6%) and ethics shares moderately lower in the deviating pair (9.7% versus 17.1%). The partition is, in plain terms, a divide between policy texts that talk about *using, integrating, and designing* AI and texts that talk about *scoping, governing, and architecting* it.

### Robustness

The Korea–Ireland grouping survives five of six pre-specified checks outright and the sixth in an informative way (fig. 2; appendix C). Weighting each document equally *strengthens* Korea's signal, because its smaller documents carry proportionally more application-skills vocabulary than its dominant 2022 curriculum; Ireland's signal moderates but stays well above the canonical mean. Bootstrap intervals for the Korea–canonical and Ireland–canonical differences on the focal anchor exclude zero by more than 17 percentage points (+27.2, 95% CI [25.5, 28.9]; +20.3, [18.0, 22.7]). Sub-sampling every country to 500 sentences preserves both assignments in 100% of 1,000 resamples; the temporal split preserves both in the pre- and post-September 2024 cohorts. Off-domain text aligns to the anchors at 0.0%. The one informative exception is leave-one-out: dropping Korea's largest document leaves Korea deviating, but dropping Ireland's largest document — the 2022 *Digital Strategy for Schools to 2027* — places Ireland in the canonical group. Korea's deviation is *document-replicable*; Ireland's is *document-singular*. The corporate AI-ethics hard-negative corpus aligns at 68.9%, marginally above the positive corpus; its adherence *distribution*, however, resembles the canonical rather than the deviating centroid (appendix C.6), so the anchors measure AI-policy vocabulary in general while the clustering still discriminates the pedagogical register.

### Korea: a curriculum-foundation commitment made in 2022

Korea's deviating signature is carried by five Ministry of Education documents spanning 2022 to 2026, and the *Application Skills* share lies between 12.5% and 31.9% in every one of them. The keystone is the 2022 *General Guidelines of the Primary and Secondary Curriculum* (Ministry of Education Notification 2022-33, Annex 1; document KR-02 in appendix A) — the foundational text that frames every subject-level curriculum in Korean schools, revised in December 2022, two years before UNESCO's framework. Its most strongly aligned sentences are not aspirational statements about AI but operational instructions to schools. The instruction that recurs across subject sections directs teachers to diagnose learners' understanding "using digital programmes such as AI-based platforms" (「디지털 프로그램(인공지능 기반 플랫폼 등)을 활용하여 학습자의 이해 능력을 진단하고」) — a sentence that treats AI as an instrument of everyday assessment practice rather than as a topic for study. The four later documents extend this curriculum-foundation logic outward: the 2023 *Digital-Based Educational Innovation Plan* into implementation strategy and the AI Digital Textbook programme; the 2024 *Classroom Revolution Lead Teachers* initiative into teacher development; the 2025 *AI for All Talent Cultivation Plan* into workforce framing; the 2026 Ministry business plan into recurrent planning. Korea's signature is therefore not a 2025 response to UNESCO 2024 but a curriculum-policy commitment that the international framework subsequently rendered legible — the *legitimation through external reference* pattern Steiner-Khamsi (2014) identifies.

### Ireland: a foundational strategy not carried forward

Ireland's deviating identity is concentrated in a single document: the *Digital Strategy for Schools to 2027* (IE-01), published by the Department of Education in April 2022, two and a half years before UNESCO's framework. Two features of the close reading matter. First, the document's strongly aligned sentences frame tool use in the vocabulary of *digital* rather than *AI* technology — learners should "make the most of the opportunities available from the use of digital technology" and use "digital learning platforms and apps including those to develop computational thinking, coding" — and it is this general tool-use register that the *Application Skills* anchor recognises. Ireland's 2022 signal is, in other words, a digital-strategy signal that the AI framework's vocabulary retrospectively captures; the polysemy Anderson-Levitt and Gardinier (2021) describe is visible here at the level of a single anchor. Second, the 2025 *Guidance on Artificial Intelligence in Schools* (IE-02), written in direct response to UNESCO 2024, does name AI tools explicitly — "AI-powered adaptive learning platforms analyse student data, such as performance and learning pace" — yet sits firmly in the canonical group, because the balance of its text turns to governance, data protection, and age restrictions. The intra-Ireland difference on the focal anchor is +21.2 percentage points (95% CI [17.2, 25.3]), comparable to the entire Korea–canonical effect. The older document carries the tool-use signature; the newer, framework-responsive document aligns with the canonical register. Both Korea and Ireland fit the *innovator/early-adopter* end of Rogers's (2003) curve — and their innovator status is visible only because the post-2024 frameworks provide a measurement instrument that renders the 2022 framing legible.

### The United States: a framework-dependent signature

The United States illustrates a third pattern. Its documents do not load on the student framework's *Application Skills* anchor (9.4%, well below the deviating pair's bootstrap floor of roughly 23%), but load heavily on the teacher framework's *AI Pedagogy* (37.1%) and *Human-Centred Mindset* (29.6%) anchors and on the OECD–EC draft's *Designing AI* (54.7%). The 2025 White House Executive Order on Advancing AI Education for American Youth and the Department of Education's 2024 AI toolkit emphasise human-agency framing and pedagogy-integration infrastructure rather than student tool-use. The United States is in the deviating group *if and only if* deviation is defined broadly as any departure from the canonical governance register, and not when it is defined narrowly as students applying AI tools. Single-framework analyses cannot surface this; a cross-national comparison conducted against one anchor framework risks attributing to a country a position that is a property of the anchor system.

## Discussion

### The canonical mass as mimetic isomorphism through soft governance

The pre-specified four-pattern hypothesis is not supported. The empirical structure is one canonical mass — 17 to 21 countries, problem-scoping and managing-AI dominant — plus a small deviating set. DiMaggio and Powell's (1983) *mimetic isomorphism* predicts exactly this: ministries drafting AI-in-education policy in 2024–2026 faced a domain with no settled national exemplar and one highly salient external reference point, and converged on its most legible register. The European soft-governance lens supplies the mechanism. A competence framework that inherits the DigComp/DigCompEdu form, feeds a PISA domain, and operationalises an AI Act obligation is a high-status exemplar of a very particular kind: its reference status is secured not by mandate but by comparability (Grek 2009), and comparability rewards the register that is easiest to compare — governance, scoping, system design. Buckner's (2019) *national interpretations of a global model* describes the same structure: what is shared is the model as reference point; what varies is the interpretation. The cross-framework replication corroborates the reading — the canonical register is recognisable under each of three independently authored anchor systems — and the Rogers heuristic accounts for the monotonic expansion of the deviating set as anchor resolution falls.

### Frontier adopters and borrowing as legitimation

The temporal evidence is the most consequential interpretive finding. In both Korea and Ireland the deviating signal is carried by 2022 documents; the 2025 Irish guidance that responds to UNESCO sits in the canonical group, and the four Korean documents that postdate the 2022 curriculum extend its vocabulary rather than borrow a new one. Korea and Ireland are *frontier adopters* whose framing ran parallel to, not downstream of, the international standardisation effort. This fits Steiner-Khamsi's (2014) *borrowing-as-legitimation*: cross-national borrowing is often less about importing source-country success than about using an external standard to legitimate prior domestic choices. Korea's 2022 curriculum embedded application-skills vocabulary; the post-2024 UNESCO framework made it internationally legible and supplied a legitimating reference for the AI Digital Textbook roll-out that Lee et al. (2025) analyse as a policy assemblage. Ireland's 2022 strategy preceded the standard; its 2025 guidance then aligned with the canonical register — a move in which the *later* document is the borrowing event. Countries that had already adopted the framing the framework later codified appear as outliers when measured against the framework's vocabulary. The Korea–Ireland asymmetry maps onto Phillips and Ochs's (2003) stages: Korea's pattern (vocabulary sustained across five documents and multiple drafting cycles) is *internalisation*; Ireland's (foundational strategy, not carried forward) is an *implementation*-stage discontinuity. The framework-dependent US signature is intelligible under the same lens as selective borrowing with national-context filtering.

### Standards travel through revision

The within-framework result reframes what cross-framework replication tests. The three anchor systems operative at harvest agree on the Korea–Ireland pair; the revised OECD–EC anchors do not, because the revision redefined the domain that had discriminated them.

A sceptical reader may object that the Korea–Ireland replication is complete only because the published framework — the one that will actually govern PISA 2029 and national implementation — is excluded from the score, and that the pair's "framework-invariant" status is therefore a draft-era artefact. Three points already in the evidence answer the objection. First, the pair's identity does not depend on the OECD–EC framework at all: it is established by the two UNESCO frameworks, independently authored by a different organisation, and by the macro-aspect profile of table 2, which uses no OECD anchor. Second, the temporal evidence — that the deviating signal is carried by documents from 2022 — is a property of the national texts, not of any anchor set, and it survives whichever anchors are used. Third, what the final anchors remove is one *additional* framework's agreement, and the paper reports that removal as a result rather than concealing it; Korea's distance from the canonical centroid halves under the revised vocabulary, and Ireland's residual position is stated with its size (table 3). Put plainly, the pre-specified replication score is 3 of 3 for the frameworks operative at harvest and 0 of 1 for the revised framework, and both numbers are informative: the first about the national texts, the second about the framework.

Three readings follow. First, the finding is a stronger demonstration of the paper's methodological claim than any between-framework disagreement could be: the *same* organisation, the *same* framework, and the *same* frozen texts yield a different national classification a year apart, so a substantial share of what a single-framework analysis reports as "Korea's position" is a property of the anchor vocabulary at a moment in time. Second, the revision is itself a moment of policy travel. The OECD–EC framework vernacularised its own fourth domain — from constructive *Designing* to critical-reflective *Shape* — in the course of consultation, and did so in a direction that national policy texts had not anticipated. Anderson-Levitt and Gardinier's (2021) polysemy operates at the level of the standard, not only of its national reception; the silences of the final framework (its near-absence of hands-on construction vocabulary in the fourth domain) are as informative as the vocabulary it amplifies. Third, the persistence of Ireland's *Shape AI* lead under the final anchors — Ireland is the only country the revised vocabulary still sets apart once the Estonian singleton is removed — is consistent with the 2022 Irish strategy's emphasis on computational thinking and learner agency having anticipated the *reflective* as well as the *instrumental* register; this is a hypothesis for the Korea–Ireland re-harvest proposed below, not a finding.

### The findings against prior work

Against Schiff (2022), whose analysis of 24 national AI strategies found education largely absent, the present results update the finding: when education becomes the explicit subject, most jurisdictions still foreground *governance* of AI rather than *pedagogy*, with Korea and Ireland the exception. Against Ng et al. (2021), Casal-Otero et al. (2023), and Mikeladze et al. (2024), whose programme has built definitional convergence at the conceptual level, the results identify a layer of policy heterogeneity the convergence literature does not anticipate: what counts as a national AI-literacy commitment depends on which sub-aspect of the converged definition a country emphasises. Against Humble's (2025) document analysis of university AI policies in this journal, which derives a dynamic alignment model from institutional guidelines, the present study is the K-12, cross-national, computational counterpart — and it adds the observation that the alignment target itself moves. Against Lee et al.'s (2025) Actor Network Theory account of Korea's AI Digital Textbook, it supplies the upstream half: the initiative appeared into a curriculum environment in which the 2022 *General Guidelines* had already embedded application-skills vocabulary.

### Implications for curriculum authorities and framework designers

The findings license four implications and one explicit non-implication.

1. *For ministries in the canonical group.* Three concept loadings separate the frontier adopters from the canonical mass — *Application Skills* (students using AI), *AI Pedagogy* (teachers integrating AI), and, under the draft OECD–EC vocabulary, *Designing AI*. National curriculum revisions intending to align with the full breadth of the frameworks, rather than only with their governance components, will need to carry such vocabulary; and the Korean and Irish cases indicate that it enters through curriculum-foundation documents, not through topical guidance issued after an international framework.

2. *For framework designers, including the OECD–EC and the PISA 2029 Media and AI Literacy construct.* A single framework can misclassify a system, and a single framework's revision can reclassify every system at once. Multi-framework triangulation — and explicit versioning of the anchor vocabulary — is a design consideration for any instrument that will be used to monitor national alignment over time.

3. *For teacher educators.* The deviating group's loading on *AI Pedagogy* rather than *AI for Professional Development* under the teacher framework points to concrete classroom integration, rather than generic AI-for-the-profession provision, as the more consequential locus of professional development.

4. *For comparative researchers.* Cross-framework replication is a portable measurement-invariance check for cross-national policy-text analysis wherever competing or evolving frameworks coexist; the replication archive supplies a worked instance.

What the analysis does *not* license is any inference from adherence in policy text to classroom practice. A high *Application Skills* share means that a national document talks in the register of students using AI; it does not mean that students do so, that teachers are prepared for it, or that the policy has been implemented. The findings are about the travel of a vocabulary, not about its enactment.

### Limitations and critical vantage

Four limitations bound the claims. The discriminant check shows that the anchors recognise AI-policy vocabulary in general, so the clustering, not the alignment rate, carries the discrimination between registers. Two small-corpus countries are excluded. The canonical corpora skew towards general AI strategies and the Korean and Irish corpora towards schooling-explicit documents, so a genre-confound reading cannot be excluded by design alone, although the temporal evidence and the cross-framework replication are strong hedges. And the corpus is frozen before the OECD–EC final framework: this is a feature for the within-framework test, since national texts could not have been written to the final wording, but a Korea–Ireland re-harvest after 2026 is the natural sequel. The analysis is descriptive; mechanism requires process-tracing of the kind Lee et al. (2025) provide.

A critical literature (Selwyn 2024; Williamson, Macgilchrist, and Potter 2023; Molenaar 2022; Cukurova 2025) reminds that treating the frameworks as a measurement instrument rather than as objects of critique is a methodological commitment, not a neutral choice. From that vantage the canonical-mass convergence is also *normalisation*: the framework's vocabulary supplies what counts as a recognisable national commitment, and jurisdictions outside it risk being read as deficient. The Korea–Ireland signature can equally be read as escape from that effect or as a different and contestable centring of AI in the classroom — a contest the *hybrid intelligence* agenda (Molenaar 2022; Cukurova 2025) makes explicit, and one that the framework's own turn towards *Shape AI* suggests its authors have joined.

## Conclusion

This article has read the post-2024 AI-in-education policy window through the comparative-education lenses of policy borrowing, diffusion of innovations, and institutional isomorphism, and through the European lens of competence frameworks as soft-governance instruments. Across the three anchor systems operative when the corpus was harvested, a stable two-group partition recurs: 21 countries occupy a canonical governance register, and Korea and Ireland alone foreground application-skills and AI-pedagogy vocabulary, in 2022 documents that predate the international frameworks. Mimetic isomorphism through soft governance accounts for the canonical mass; Rogers's innovator category and Steiner-Khamsi's borrowing-as-legitimation account for the frontier pair and for the temporal direction of their signature; Phillips and Ochs's stages distinguish Korea's internalised from Ireland's discontinued adoption. The OECD–EC framework's own revision then shows that standards travel through their revision as well as into national systems: the redefinition of the fourth domain from *Designing* to *Shape AI* dissolves the axis that had discriminated the frontier pair. For curriculum authorities the loadings that matter, and the documents through which they enter, are now identifiable; for framework designers, including those specifying the PISA 2029 Media and AI Literacy construct, the case for multi-framework triangulation and explicit anchor versioning is made empirically. Identifying which register a national document occupies, against which framework, and at which version of it, is a tractable empirical question — and a substantively informative one about how international standards travel.
