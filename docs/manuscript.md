# Tool-use as deviation: A cross-framework sentence-embedding analysis of national AI-in-education policy adoption in 25 countries

**[Author information removed for anonymous peer review]**

## Abstract

Between September 2024 and May 2025, UNESCO and the OECD–European Commission issued three AI competency frameworks for school education, supplying the field with a standardised conceptual architecture for measuring national education-AI policy text. This article asks how 25 national AI-in-education policy texts harvested in May 2026 align with that architecture, and whether alignment patterns replicate across the three frameworks. Every sentence of the 67-document corpus is aligned against the verbatim anchor sentences of the UNESCO Student (twelve anchors), UNESCO Teacher (five), and OECD–European Commission AI Literacy (four) frameworks using a multilingual sentence-embedding model; the resulting country-level adherence distributions are clustered using Hellinger distance and validated by a pre-registered six-module robustness battery. A stable two-cluster partition emerges: 21 countries occupy a canonical cluster dominated by problem-scoping and managing-AI vocabulary, and a small deviating cluster is characterised by application-skills, AI-pedagogy, and designing-AI vocabulary. Korea and Ireland are alone in the deviating cluster under every framework tested. Both signatures predate the UNESCO 2024 framework: Korea's signal is carried primarily by its 2022 national curriculum standards (KR-02), and Ireland's by its 2022 *Digital Strategy for Schools to 2027* (IE-01), suggesting that both countries are frontier adopters whose pedagogical framing of AI tool-use ran parallel to, not downstream of, the international standardisation effort. The cross-framework replication design supplies a generalisable methodology for distinguishing framework-invariant from framework-specific findings in cross-national education-policy text analysis, with direct implications for curriculum designers and teacher educators applying the UNESCO and OECD–European Commission anchor systems.

**Keywords:** AI literacy; AI competency frameworks; cross-national policy analysis; sentence-embedding alignment; hierarchical clustering; diffusion of innovations

# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 The arrival of three AI competency frameworks for school education

Within an eighteen-month window beginning in September 2024, the world's two principal multilateral education-policy organisations issued three AI competency frameworks for school education. UNESCO published the AI Competency Framework for Students (Miao & Shiohira, 2024a) and the AI Competency Framework for Teachers (Miao & Shiohira, 2024b) at its 2024 Digital Learning Week. The OECD and the European Commission jointly released the May 2025 review draft of *Empowering Learners for the Age of AI: An AI Literacy Framework for Primary and Secondary Education* (OECD & European Commission, 2025), with the final version expected by the close of 2026. Each framework offers a structured taxonomy of the knowledge, skills, attitudes, and ethical orientations that primary and secondary learners and their teachers are expected to develop in the era of generative and predictive AI. Each framework also functions, by intent of its publishers, as a benchmark against which national curricula and policies may be reviewed and revised. The frameworks arrive at a moment when the field of AI in education has expanded rapidly along both technical and pedagogical dimensions (Crompton & Burke, 2023; Holmes & Tuomi, 2022), and when the arrival of generative AI has thrown into sharp relief the absence of a shared conceptual vocabulary across national jurisdictions (Lim et al., 2023; Williamson et al., 2023).

These framework releases have coincided with an unusual concentration of national policy activity on AI in education. National-level documents — strategies, curricular guidance, teacher-development plans, and supplementary curricula — are being drafted, revised, and published across diverse jurisdictions at a pace that has few precedents in twenty-first-century education policy. The question this article addresses is how those national documents align with the conceptual architecture supplied by UNESCO and the OECD–European Commission: which framework concepts are foregrounded, which are absent, and whether national patterns of adherence resolve into a small number of identifiable alignment signatures that have direct implications for curriculum design and teacher professional development in the immediate post-framework period.

## 1.2 The gap: cross-national measurement of framework adoption

Several constraints currently limit researchers' ability to answer that question. First, the primary documents are dispersed across national ministry portals in eight languages and are rarely indexed in standard policy databases. Second, the closest prior study of cross-national AI policy text analysed national AI strategies rather than education-specific policies and found that the instrumental value of education in supporting an AI-ready workforce dominated those documents, while AI in education itself was largely absent and the ethical implications of AI in educational settings received scant attention (Schiff, 2022). That study examined 24 English-language national AI strategy documents issued between 2016 and the beginning of 2020 using frame-based content analysis; it identified the "Education for AI, not AI for Education" pattern that has come to define the literature on this topic. The Schiff (2022) finding raises a follow-up question that is not yet addressed in the published literature: when policy documents are *explicitly* about education and artificial intelligence — that is, when the educational dimension is the subject of the document rather than an instrumental subsection of a broader AI strategy — does the same pattern hold, or does a different conceptual architecture emerge?

Third, even where such documents exist, the field of AI literacy itself is conceptually contested. Long and Magerko (2020) offered a foundational taxonomy of sixteen AI literacy competencies and design considerations. Ng et al. (2021) reviewed thirty peer-reviewed papers and proposed a four-aspect schema — know-and-understand, use-and-apply, evaluate-and-create, and ethical issues — that anticipates UNESCO's later four-aspect student framework with considerable structural overlap. Casal-Otero et al. (2023), in a systematic literature review of K–12 AI literacy interventions, documented a field characterised by methodological diversity and limited cross-study comparability. Adjacent reviews of AI in education more broadly (Zhai et al., 2021; Zhang & Aslan, 2021) and of K-12 AI learning tools specifically (Yim & Su, 2024) reach a parallel conclusion: a substantial body of curricular and tool-development work exists, but its theoretical grounding has lacked an agreed reference architecture. The arrival of UNESCO's and the OECD–European Commission's frameworks supplies, in principle, the standardised conceptual architecture that the field has lacked. Whether that architecture is reflected in national policy text remains an open empirical question.

## 1.3 Approach and research questions

This article addresses that question through a computational text analysis of 25 national AI-in-education policy texts harvested in May 2026. The analytical strategy aligns every corpus sentence against the verbatim anchor sentences of three independent AI competency frameworks — UNESCO student (twelve anchors), UNESCO teacher (five anchors), and OECD–European Commission (four anchors) — using multilingual sentence embeddings, and then clusters the resulting country-level adherence distributions using Hellinger distance. The cross-framework design is deliberate: it asks whether any cluster structure recovered from the principal UNESCO student analysis is preserved when the analysis is re-run against the other two frameworks, and thereby distinguishes framework-invariant adoption signatures from artefacts of any single anchor space.

Four research questions structure the analysis. RQ1: What cluster structure emerges from Hellinger-distance clustering of the 25-country adherence matrix against the UNESCO Student Framework's twelve anchors? RQ2: Does this cluster structure replicate when the analysis is re-run against the UNESCO teacher and OECD–European Commission frameworks? RQ3: Does the cluster identity of any country survive a six-module robustness battery comprising inverse-corpus weighting, leave-one-out, bootstrap confidence intervals, uniform-cap sub-sampling, temporal cohort split, and discriminant validity? RQ4: How does the resulting pattern of cluster identity map onto diffusion-of-innovation typologies of national policy adoption?

## 1.4 Contributions

This article makes three contributions to the literature on AI in education. First, it documents a stable K = 2 partition in which Korea and Ireland constitute a deviating cluster characterised by adherence to the UNESCO Student Framework's Application Skills anchor, while the remaining 21 sample countries occupy a canonical cluster anchored on Problem Scoping. The deviating cluster identity replicates across all three anchor frameworks tested (two of which — the UNESCO student and teacher frameworks — share authorship), and the partition survives the six-module robustness battery. The asymmetry between Korea (a document-replicable deviation distributed across five Ministry of Education documents from 2022 to 2026) and Ireland (a document-singular deviation concentrated in the 2022 *Digital Strategy for Schools to 2027*) emerges as a substantive sub-finding rather than as a methodological inconsistency. Both signatures predate the UNESCO 2024 framework, recasting Korea and Ireland as frontier adopters whose pedagogy-of-AI framings ran parallel to, rather than downstream of, the international standardisation effort.

Second, the cross-framework replication design supplies a generalisable methodological template for distinguishing framework-invariant from framework-specific findings in cross-national policy text analysis. The procedure — re-run the principal pipeline against each independent anchor framework, report framework-pair cluster-identity agreement, and compute a country-level replication score — is portable to other policy domains where competing institutional frameworks coexist.

Third, the analysis revises the pre-registered four-pattern bifurcation hypothesis that had been derived from a five-country pilot. The 25-country data do not support a four-cluster decomposition; instead, one canonical cluster dominates (with approximately three-quarters of the sample) and a small deviating cluster (Korea and Ireland in the principal analysis; expanding to {Korea, United States, Ireland} under the UNESCO teacher framework and to a six-country set under the OECD framework) departs from it. This nested expansion across frameworks of decreasing dimensionality is itself interpretable through the diffusion-of-innovation typology developed in §2.

The article proceeds as follows. Section 2 develops the theoretical framework. Section 3 details the corpus, anchors, alignment procedure, clustering, robustness battery, cross-framework replication, and reproducibility infrastructure. Section 4 reports the principal results and the robustness battery. Section 5 discusses the findings against the theoretical framework and identifies limitations. Section 6 concludes.

# 2. Theoretical framework

## 2.1 AI literacy as a structured construct

The construct of AI literacy has matured rapidly since Long and Magerko's (2020) foundational taxonomy of sixteen competencies — recognising the presence of AI, understanding its strengths and limits, communicating with AI, programming AI, and so on — was published in the proceedings of the 2020 CHI Conference. The Long and Magerko taxonomy treats AI literacy as a multidimensional construct combining cognitive, ethical, and practical competencies, structured by HCI-derived design considerations rather than by curricular sequence. A parallel North American effort by Touretzky, Gardner-McCune, Martin, and Seehorn (2019) framed the same construct as "Five Big Ideas in AI for K-12" (perception, representation and reasoning, learning, natural interaction, and societal impact), providing the curricular sequencing that the Long and Magerko taxonomy left under-specified. Ng et al. (2021), reviewing thirty peer-reviewed studies, recast the construct into a four-aspect schema — know-and-understand, use-and-apply, evaluate-and-create, and ethical issues — that anticipates the four-aspect structure subsequently adopted by UNESCO's Student Framework: human-centred mindset, ethics of AI, AI techniques and applications, and AI system design. The structural convergence between Ng et al. (2021) and Miao and Shiohira (2024a) is striking and suggests that, at the macro-aspect level at which the present analysis operates, the underlying conceptual architecture of school-targeted AI literacy has reached a degree of definitional stability that the Casal-Otero et al. (2023) systematic review concluded was, in 2022, still emerging.

Empirical work building on these taxonomies has expanded substantially since 2021. Druga, Vu, Likhith, and Qiu (2019) examined inclusive AI literacy interventions across multiple national settings, foregrounding equity and participation as central concerns. Kong, Cheung, and Zhang (2021) evaluated a multi-week AI literacy course delivered to university students from diverse disciplinary backgrounds and documented measurable competency gains on both technical and ethical dimensions. Su, Ng, and Chu (2023) extended this empirical programme into early childhood education, identifying both the developmental affordances of AI literacy at younger ages and the practical constraints on teacher capacity. Chiu (2023) proposed a holistic design framework integrating curriculum, pedagogy, assessment, and teacher development for K-12 AI education, and Wang, Rau, and Yuan (2023) developed and validated a psychometric scale for measuring user AI literacy competence. Yim and Su (2024), in a scoping review of K-12 AI learning tools, documented the rapid expansion of the tool ecosystem and the corresponding need for curriculum-level frames that organise tool use within coherent pedagogical sequences. These empirical and design contributions converge with the Ng et al. (2021) and Long and Magerko (2020) taxonomies in identifying AI literacy as a multidimensional construct, while supplying the curricular sequencing, age-grading, and measurement instruments that the foundational taxonomies left implicit.

This convergence licenses the methodological choice central to the present analysis: treating the UNESCO and OECD–European Commission anchors as a stable measurement instrument rather than as one more contestable framework among many. Where measurement-instrument stability is uncontroversial, attention can shift from the construction of the anchors to the patterns of national alignment against them.

## 2.2 Three frameworks, three concept spaces

The three anchor frameworks differ in dimensionality and orientation. The UNESCO Student Framework's twelve anchors are organised in a four-aspect by three-progression-level matrix (understand, apply, create) and are pitched at the level of student-facing competencies (Miao & Shiohira, 2024a). The UNESCO Teacher Framework's five anchors collapse the four aspects into five teacher-facing macro-categories that include AI Pedagogy and AI for Professional Development, categories with no direct counterpart in the student framework (Miao & Shiohira, 2024b). The OECD–European Commission AI Literacy Framework reorganises the construct into four domains pitched at the level of learner activity rather than competency type: engaging with, creating, managing, and designing AI (OECD & European Commission, 2025). The three frameworks address overlapping conceptual territory but partition it differently, and their independent publication by two organisations provides an opportunity to test whether national policy adoption patterns are anchored to the territory itself or to the way any one framework partitions it.

## 2.3 Diffusion and isomorphism in cross-national policy adoption

National responses to international policy initiatives have been theorised under three complementary lenses, each of which contributes to the present analysis. Rogers (2003), in the fifth edition of *Diffusion of Innovations*, distinguished innovators and early adopters from early majority, late majority, and laggards along a normal-curve adoption distribution and identified the social-system properties — communication channels, opinion leadership, and relative advantage — that shape diffusion speed. Applied to cross-national education-policy adoption, the Rogers typology suggests a three-tier reading of the sample: a small set of frontier adopters whose policy text predates or coincides with the framework publication and whose framing converges with the framework on substantive grounds, a larger middle tier of fast followers whose policy text post-dates the framework and adopts a portion of its vocabulary, and a residual set of recipients whose adoption is more nominal than substantive.

DiMaggio and Powell (1983) offered a second lens centred not on speed of adoption but on the *mechanism* of isomorphism — the process by which actors in an organisational field come to resemble one another. They distinguished coercive isomorphism (driven by external mandate), mimetic isomorphism (driven by uncertainty and the imitation of high-status models), and normative isomorphism (driven by professional socialisation). The post-2024 wave of national AI-in-education policy activity exhibits features consistent with all three mechanisms. UNESCO and OECD framework publication functions normatively; international ranking and benchmarking pressure functions coercively; and the absence of established national models in this rapidly emerging field encourages mimetic adoption of frontier exemplars.

Comparative-education scholarship on *policy borrowing* supplies a third, more granular lens. Phillips and Ochs (2003) distinguished four stages in cross-national policy borrowing — attraction (external initiative becomes attractive to potential borrowers), decision (formal decision to borrow), implementation (translation into national context), and internalisation (incorporation into national practice and the development of indigenous meanings) — and emphasised that the *translation* stage typically transforms the borrowed policy in ways that the originating jurisdiction did not anticipate. Steiner-Khamsi (2014) developed this into a framework for understanding reception and translation in cross-national policy mobility, foregrounding the role of national context in shaping how an external policy text is interpreted, redacted, and selectively adopted. Ball (2003) and Verger, Steiner-Khamsi, and Lubienski (2017) further situated such borrowing within the broader political-economy context of global education governance, in which national jurisdictions both produce and consume policy text under conditions of asymmetric institutional power. The combined diffusion-isomorphism-borrowing lens predicts that, in the immediate post-2024 framework window, the most legible cross-national variation will lie not in whether countries adhere to the framework concepts at all (the macro-aspect convergence is high, as Ng et al. 2021 anticipated) but in which sub-aspects are foregrounded and which are silently translated, redacted, or replaced. The empirical question this article addresses is whether any such patterned variation is identifiable in the cross-national data.

## 2.4 The Korean case and the four-pattern hypothesis

Korea's *AI Digital Textbook* (AIDT) initiative, launched as a Ministry of Education flagship in 2023 and rolled out across selected subjects in 2025, has been the subject of recent critical scholarship as an exemplary case of EdTech policy assemblage in a frontier-adopter jurisdiction. Lee et al. (2025) traced the AIDT initiative's lifecycle from ideation through stakeholder mobilisation, institutional inscription, contestation, and partial reclassification, drawing on Actor Network Theory to characterise the policy as the unstable product of shifting human and non-human alignments. For present purposes the Lee et al. analysis matters as theoretical context: Korea has, over roughly the same period in which the international frameworks were being drafted, committed substantial state resources to a tool-use-oriented AI-in-education policy programme. If the diffusion and isomorphism lenses jointly apply, Korea ought to appear as a frontier adopter whose policy text exhibits a tool-use-oriented framing irrespective of which anchor framework is used.

A pre-registered hypothesis was developed during the pilot phase of the present study. Five pilot countries (Korea, the United Kingdom, Singapore, Finland, and the United States) suggested four possible adoption signatures: tool-use, system-design, ethics-led, and human-agency. The pilot interpretation hypothesised that these four signatures would resolve into four roughly equal clusters in the full 25-country analysis. The 25-country results reported in §4 do not support this four-cluster decomposition. The revision of the hypothesis — from a four-pattern bifurcation to a one-canonical-cluster-plus-one-deviating-cluster structure — is itself a finding of the analysis and is developed in §5.

# 3. Methods

This section describes the corpus construction, anchor extraction and validation, multilingual text pipeline, embedding and alignment procedure, adherence matrix construction, Hellinger-distance clustering, six-module robustness battery, cross-framework replication design, and reproducibility infrastructure. The analytical strategy was pre-specified in a planning document drafted in May 2026 (file `AILIT_TRAVEL_planning_v1.md` in the project repository); deviations from that plan, where they occur, are flagged in the corresponding subsection.

## 3.1 Sampling frame and corpus selection

The sampling frame comprises 25 countries, selected to provide coverage of (a) all four OECD regional groupings (Europe, Asia–Pacific, Americas, Middle East and Africa), (b) a range of GDP-per-capita quartiles, (c) a mix of pre- and post-September 2024 publication dates relative to the UNESCO AI competency framework release, and (d) at least one publicly accessible national AI-in-education policy document per country. The list of countries was finalised before alignment results were inspected.

Sixty-eight candidate documents were harvested between 28 and 30 May 2026; one Chinese-language scanned PDF (CN-03) was dropped before analysis because Chinese-language OCR was outside the pipeline's automated capabilities, leaving an analytic corpus of 67 documents (see §4.1 and the limitations note in §5.6). Inclusion criteria, set during the planning phase, required that each document (i) be a national government, ministry, government-commissioned report, or a verified third-party mirror of such a document (notably OECD AI Observatory dashboard pages where the original government URL had been retired); (ii) address artificial intelligence either explicitly in the educational context or with substantive implications for education; and (iii) be available in full text in HTML, PDF, or .docx form from a verifiable institutional source. Source-type classifications (government primary; government-commissioned; verified third-party mirror) are provided per document in Appendix A. Eight documents that initially failed automated extraction were either replaced with alternative sources from the same publisher (n = 5; e.g., ZA-01 substituted from the South African Department of Communications portal page to the gov.za direct PDF) or extracted manually by the author (n = 3; e.g., DE-02 KI-Aktionsplan from the BMBF press release).

The corpus design choice to focus on AI-in-education policy texts specifically, rather than general national AI strategies, distinguishes the present sample from the 24-country corpus analysed by Schiff (2022), whose study found that the instrumental value of education in supporting an AI-ready workforce dominated national AI strategy documents while AI-in-education itself was largely absent. The present corpus inverts that focus and asks how the educational dimension is articulated when it is the explicit subject of the document.

## 3.2 Verbatim anchor extraction

Three framework documents supplied the anchor sentences against which corpus sentences were aligned. The UNESCO AI Competency Framework for Students (Miao & Shiohira, 2024a) provided twelve anchors organised into a 4-aspect × 3-level matrix (human-centred mindset, ethics of AI, AI techniques and applications, AI system design, each at understand, apply, and create levels). The UNESCO AI Competency Framework for Teachers (Miao & Shiohira, 2024b) provided five anchors corresponding to the five macro-aspects, in the framework's official order: T01 human-centred mindset, T02 ethics of AI, T03 AI foundations and applications, T04 AI pedagogy, and T05 AI for professional development. The OECD–European Commission AI Literacy Framework Review Draft of May 2025 (OECD & European Commission, 2025) provided four anchors corresponding to the four domains (engaging with AI, creating AI, managing AI, designing AI). Because the two UNESCO frameworks share authorship (Miao and Shiohira), the cross-framework replication design tests two-organisation rather than three-organisation independence; the implications of this design choice are discussed in §4.5.5.

Anchors were extracted verbatim from each framework's principal definition paragraph for each sub-principle. The verbatim principle excluded paraphrasing or summarising of the framework text. When verbatim extraction produced two adjacent anchors with insufficient cohesive separation (defined in §3.3), the affected anchor was rewritten using only material drawn from the same framework section; these revised anchors are flagged in the project's anchor CSV files with the status `verbatim_revised`, and the rationale for each revision is documented in an anchor-revision rationale document (released in the replication archive). Three of the twelve student anchors (S04, S05, S06) were revised under this procedure; the original versions are archived for sensitivity analysis under the tag `v1_archived`.

## 3.3 Anchor cohesion validation

Anchor cohesion was operationalised as the ratio of mean cosine distance within an aspect to mean cosine distance between adjacent aspects, computed on the 21-anchor distance matrix. Cohesion ratios above 1.05 were treated as adequate; below 1.00 indicated that anchors within the same aspect were further apart in the embedding space than anchors in adjacent aspects, a failure mode requiring revision. Under the v1 student anchors, AILIT-S04 and AILIT-S06 returned ratios of 0.97; revising those anchors and AILIT-S05 (Path A; the revision rationale is documented in the replication archive) yielded v2 ratios of 1.16, 1.08, and 1.04 respectively. All three anchor frameworks (student v2, teacher, OECD) passed the cohesion threshold at the macro-aspect level. The full 21 × 21 cosine distance matrix is provided in Appendix B.

## 3.4 Text extraction and sentence segmentation

Documents were extracted using `pdfminer.six` 20240706 for PDFs, `trafilatura` 1.6.0 for HTML (with `include_tables=True` and `favor_recall=True`), and `python-docx` 1.2.0 for the one Australian .docx document. For one Mexican PDF (MX-01, 143 pages, custom CID fonts without a Unicode cmap), automated text extraction returned glyph codes rather than readable characters; this document was re-extracted using a `pdftoppm` to `tesseract` OCR pipeline at 150 dpi resolution. After excluding the dropped CN-03 scanned PDF (§3.1), eight languages are represented in the resulting 67-document corpus: English, Korean, German, French, Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, and Japanese.

Sentence segmentation used a multilingual regular expression matching terminal punctuation (`.!?。`) followed by whitespace, with a length filter retaining sentences between 40 and 600 characters. This range was selected based on a prior validation by the present author against a national education-policy reference corpus, which found that this window captured policy-relevant sentences while excluding headings (under 40 characters) and unsegmented blocks (over 600 characters). The full 67-document corpus produced 36,098 sentences after segmentation.

## 3.5 Sentence embedding and alignment

Embeddings were computed using `sentence-transformers/paraphrase-multilingual-mpnet-base-v2` (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019), a 109-million-parameter model trained on 50 languages that produces 768-dimensional sentence vectors. Each sentence vector was L2-normalised so that cosine similarity reduces to a dot product. Alignment proceeded by computing the cosine similarity between every corpus sentence and every anchor, then assigning each sentence to the anchor with maximum similarity. Assignments below a pre-specified threshold of 0.35 were classified as unaligned and excluded from the adherence matrix.

The threshold of 0.35 was inherited from the present author's prior validation work on national education policy texts and was specified before alignment was executed on the present corpus. A sensitivity sweep across thresholds 0.30, 0.35, 0.40, 0.45, and 0.50 is reported in §4.3.

The methodological strategy of aligning policy or curriculum sentences against framework anchors via sentence embeddings situates the present analysis within the broader "text as data" tradition in social-science research. Grimmer and Stewart (2013) outlined the methodological promise and pitfalls of automated text analysis for political and policy texts, with particular emphasis on the risks of over-fitting and the need for explicit validation against domain expert judgement. Wilkerson and Casas (2017) updated this account for the era of large-scale text corpora and supervised learning, and Gentzkow, Kelly, and Taddy (2019) extended it further to economic and policy applications. Most directly relevant to the present study, Rodriguez and Spirling (2022) examined the strengths and weaknesses of word- and sentence-embedding approaches for applied social-science research, identifying construct validity and embedding-space drift as the principal concerns and recommending domain-specific validation against pre-registered semantic targets. The present analysis applies these recommendations through the anchor-cohesion validation (§3.3), the threshold sensitivity sweep (§4.3), and the six-module robustness battery (§3.8) described below.

Within educational measurement specifically, Butterfuss and Doran (2025) used a closely related sentence-embedding approach to support content mapping of state academic standards against framework taxonomies, demonstrating that pre-trained transformer embeddings can complement subject-matter expert judgements in standards-alignment work. The present design extends this approach from within-jurisdiction standards alignment to cross-national policy alignment analysis, and adds a six-module robustness battery (§3.8) and a cross-framework replication design (§3.9) that, to the present author's knowledge, have not previously been applied together to AI-in-education policy text.

## 3.6 Adherence matrix construction

For each country c and each anchor a, the count of corpus sentences from c assigned to a was tallied and divided by the total number of aligned sentences from c, yielding a row-normalised percentage. The resulting 25 × *N* adherence matrix (where *N* is 12 for the UNESCO student framework, 5 for UNESCO teacher, 4 for OECD) is the primary input to the clustering analysis in §3.7. By-document adherence matrices (67 × *N*) were also produced to support the leave-one-out and bootstrap analyses described in §3.8. All matrices are released as comma-separated value files in the data repository (§3.10).

## 3.7 Hellinger distance clustering and silhouette

Each row of the adherence matrix can be interpreted as a probability distribution over the *N* anchors. Pairwise distances between countries were computed using the Hellinger distance:

H(p, q) = (1/√2) · √[ Σ_i (√p_i − √q_i)² ]                                (Eq. 1)

which is bounded in [0, 1] and treats the anchor distribution as a categorical distribution. The Hellinger distance was selected over Jensen–Shannon, total variation, and cosine alternatives because it is a proper metric on the probability simplex, symmetric, and standard for clustering categorical-distribution data.

The 25 × 25 (or 23 × 23 after the *N* ≥ 50 alignment-count filter described below) distance matrix was then submitted to agglomerative hierarchical clustering using both Ward and average linkage as implemented in `scipy.cluster.hierarchy`. Silhouette scores were computed for K ∈ {2, 3, 4, 5, 6} using `sklearn.metrics.silhouette_score` with `metric='precomputed'` against the Hellinger distance matrix. The K value maximising the silhouette score under Ward linkage was reported as the principal partition; agreement between Ward and average linkage at the optimal K was reported as a secondary check.

Countries with fewer than 50 aligned sentences under a given anchor framework were excluded from the clustering analysis for that framework, on the grounds that Hellinger distance computed on a distribution with very few observations is dominated by sampling noise rather than substantive content. Under the UNESCO student framework this filter excluded the United Arab Emirates (n = 7) and Australia (n = 42); under the UNESCO teacher framework it additionally excluded Estonia (n = 49); under the OECD framework it excluded the United Arab Emirates (n = 6) and Australia (n = 32).

## 3.8 Robustness battery: six pre-specified modules

Six robustness checks were specified before alignment results were inspected, motivated by the methodological criteria for cross-national text-analytic studies pre-specified in the author's planning document (released in the replication archive). The six modules address distinct sources of artefactual cluster identity.

*Module 1 — Inverse-corpus weighting* recomputes each country's adherence row as the unweighted mean of its per-document rows, addressing concerns that single dominant documents (notably KR-02 at 89 % of Korea's aligned-sentence count, FI-01 at 98 % of Finland's, and FR-03 at 73 % of France's) may drive country-level cluster assignment.

*Module 2 — Leave-one-out per country* drops each country's largest single document and recomputes the cluster assignment using only the remaining documents, addressing concerns that cluster identity depends on one specific document rather than the country's overall policy posture.

*Module 3 — Bootstrap 95 % confidence intervals* resamples aligned sentences with replacement within each country, 1,000 iterations with seed 20260530, and reports percentile-method confidence intervals on per-country focal-anchor shares and on the difference between focal-country shares and the canonical-cluster mean.

*Module 4 — Uniform-cap sub-sampling* caps every country at *N* = 500 randomly-sampled aligned sentences per iteration, 1,000 iterations, and reports the cluster-assignment rate for each country across resamples. This module is the most aggressive size-correction available in the protocol.

*Module 5 — Temporal cohort split* partitions the corpus into pre- and post-September 2024 sub-corpora (corresponding to the UNESCO student framework release) and recomputes cluster assignments separately in each cohort, addressing the alternative explanation that cluster identity reflects the timing of policy text drafting relative to UNESCO's publication.

*Module 6 — Discriminant validity* aligns two negative corpora against the UNESCO student anchors: an off-domain set comprising literary fiction, military history, and biology (3,271 sentences) and a hard-negative set comprising corporate AI policy documents (328 sentences). Alignment rates substantially below the positive-corpus rate would support the construct validity of the anchor-based measurement.

## 3.9 Cross-framework replication

The cross-framework replication design re-runs the full pipeline (§§3.4–3.7) using the UNESCO teacher anchors and then the OECD AILit anchors as primary anchor spaces. This produces, for each anchor framework, an independent K = 2 cluster partition. Two summary statistics are reported. First, the framework-pair agreement rate is the proportion of eligible countries assigned to the same cluster identity by both frameworks of a given pair. Second, the country-level replication score is the count of frameworks (out of three) under which a country falls in the deviating cluster (defined as the cluster containing Korea and Ireland in the principal UNESCO student analysis). This design, to the author's knowledge, has not previously been applied to AI competency framework adoption analysis.

## 3.10 Reproducibility infrastructure

The full analytical pipeline is implemented as a series of shell-orchestrated Python scripts in the project repository. Every step is idempotent: re-running a step with existing output files leaves them unchanged. A single shell command (see the replication archive's `run_pipeline.sh` or equivalent entry-point) reproduces the principal UNESCO student analysis end to end, including text extraction, sentence segmentation, embedding, alignment, adherence matrix construction, and dominant-aspect summary. Separate one-line shell wrappers reproduce the cross-framework replication and each robustness module. All code, corpus inventory (with verifiable source URLs and access dates), anchor CSV files, and per-document alignment outputs are released under an open license; the persistent identifier and access URL are provided in the Data Availability statement at the end of this article.

# 4. Results

## 4.1 Corpus characteristics

The final analytic corpus comprises 67 documents from 25 countries, totalling 36,098 sentences after segmentation. The corpus represents eight languages, distributed as English (47 documents, including English-language editions of supranational dashboards and OECD AI Observatory dashboard pages), Korean (5), Spanish (4), French (3), German (3), Portuguese (3), Italian (1), and Japanese (1). The single Chinese-language scanned document (CN-03) that was harvested but dropped from analysis is documented in §3.1. Document genres include national AI strategies, ministry-of-education curriculum guidance, AI-in-schools frameworks, parliamentary research briefs, and one supranational coordination document (Netherlands' submission to the EU AI Alliance). The earliest document in the corpus is China's 2017 State Council New Generation AI Development Plan; the most recent are South Korea's 2026 Ministry of Education business plan and France's 2025 *L'IA en éducation* curriculum guidance. Twenty-nine documents in the corpus were issued after the UNESCO student framework's September 2024 publication and are therefore eligible for the §4.6 temporal cohort analysis. Document provenance for every corpus entry, including source URL, access date, file size, original publishing body, and source type (government primary; government-commissioned; verified third-party mirror), is reported in Appendix A.

## 4.2 Anchor cohesion validation

The 21-anchor cohesion analysis described in §3.3 returned within-aspect to between-aspect cosine distance ratios above the 1.05 threshold for all five UNESCO teacher anchors and all four OECD anchors. Under the version-one (archived) UNESCO student anchors, two of the four Ethics aspect anchors (S04 and S06) returned ratios below 1.00, indicating cohesion failure. The version-two revisions reported in §3.2 (Path A; the revision rationale is documented in the replication archive) raised these ratios to 1.16 (S04), 1.08 (S05), and 1.04 (S06). The principal analysis uses the version-two anchors; §4.6 reports a sensitivity analysis using the archived version-one anchors. The full cohesion matrix and per-anchor ratios are reported in Appendix B.

## 4.3 Alignment yield and threshold sensitivity

At the pre-specified alignment threshold of 0.35 cosine similarity, the principal analysis yielded 23,961 aligned sentences from a denominator of 35,873 segmented sentences (the denominator excludes the 225 sentences from South Africa's second document, ZA-02, which was processed incrementally after the principal alignment run). The overall alignment rate of 66.8% under version-two student anchors compares with 68.4% under version-one anchors. Per-country alignment rates range from 19.6% (Spain, attributable to ES-01's high proportion of administrative boilerplate) to 94.1% (Canada's CIFAR document), with a sample-weighted median of 71.7%. Pre-specified threshold sensitivity at 0.30, 0.35, 0.40, 0.45, and 0.50 returned monotonically decreasing alignment rates (39.3%, 27.8%, 19.6%, 13.6%, 9.3% at the pilot-corpus stage) while preserving the relative ordering of country-level adherence shares. The aligned-sentence-count distribution across countries is unbalanced: Korea contributes 3,297 aligned sentences and the United States 3,211, while the United Arab Emirates contributes 7 and Australia 42. Two countries with aligned-sentence counts below the §3.7 threshold of 50 (UAE and Australia) are excluded from the clustering analysis but reported in the dominant-aspect tabulation in §4.4 with an explicit small-corpus flag.

## 4.4 Dominant-aspect distributions

Aggregating the twelve student anchors into the four UNESCO aspects (human-centred mindset, ethics of AI, AI techniques and applications, AI system design) by simple summation yields a dominant-aspect classification for each country. Seventeen of the 25 countries (the United Kingdom, Singapore, China, Japan, Germany, France, Canada, Israel, India, Estonia, Norway, Sweden, the Netherlands, Spain, Italy, Saudi Arabia, and Brazil) are dominant on AI System Design, with within-row shares ranging from 34.3% (Norway) to 60.4% (China). Four countries (the United States, Ireland, Mexico, and South Africa) are dominant on Human-Centred Mindset, with shares ranging from 32.5% to 37.8%. Finland is the only country dominant on Ethics of AI (28.7%), and Korea is the only country dominant on AI Techniques and Applications (42.8%). The two small-corpus countries (UAE and Australia) are flagged separately and are not interpreted as substantive dominant-aspect cases. Per-country adherence percentages across all twelve student anchors, the five teacher anchors, and the four OECD anchors are reported in Tables 1, 2, and 3 respectively.

The aggregation to four aspects therefore returns a distribution heavily skewed toward AI System Design, with three smaller groups (Human-Centred Mindset, Ethics of AI, AI Techniques and Applications) accounting for the remainder. This distribution does not, however, map directly onto the K = 2 cluster partition recovered by Hellinger clustering, which is reported in §4.5. Korea and Ireland — the two countries that constitute the deviating cluster in the principal analysis — are dominant on different aspects under the four-aspect aggregation (Korea on Techniques and Applications, Ireland on Human-Centred Mindset). The clustering analysis therefore identifies a structural similarity between Korea and Ireland that is not visible at the level of dominant-aspect classification, a point developed further in §4.5 and §5.


## 4.5 Cross-framework replication of the K = 2 deviating cluster

### 4.5.1 K = 2 partition on the UNESCO student framework

Hierarchical clustering of the 25 country adherence rows on Hellinger distances over the 12 UNESCO student anchors yields a stable two-cluster partition. After excluding the United Arab Emirates (*n* = 7 aligned sentences) and Australia (*n* = 42) by the pre-registered minimum-corpus threshold of *N* ≥ 50 (§3.7), the K = 2 silhouette is 0.379 under Ward linkage and 0.379 under average linkage, both higher than every K ∈ {3, 4, 5, 6} examined (see Appendix D for the full K = 2 to K = 6 silhouette comparison). The deviating cluster comprises Korea and Ireland; the canonical cluster contains the remaining 21 countries.

The deviating cluster's centroid loads heavily on AILIT-S08 (Application skills); KR's S08 share is 30.2 %, IE's is 23.3 %, and the canonical-cluster mean is 3.05 % (bootstrap 95 % CI [2.78, 3.37], §4.6 Module 3). The KR − canonical effect size is +27.19 percentage points (95 % CI [25.53, 28.89]) and the IE − canonical effect size is +20.33 percentage points (95 % CI [18.04, 22.74]). Both confidence intervals exclude zero by more than 17 percentage points; the cluster separation is therefore statistically unambiguous at the resolution at which embedding-based text clustering operates.

### 4.5.2 Replication on the UNESCO teacher framework

To test whether the K = 2 partition is an artefact of the student-framework concept space, the 25-country corpus was re-aligned against the five UNESCO teacher anchors in their official order: T01 human-centred mindset, T02 ethics of AI, T03 AI foundations and applications, T04 AI pedagogy, and T05 AI for professional development. After the same *n* ≥ 50 filter (which here additionally excludes Estonia at *n* = 49), the K = 2 Ward silhouette on the 22 remaining countries is 0.473, substantially higher than the student-framework value. The deviating cluster is `{KR, US, IE}`. Korea retains its position as the most distant single-country point in the dendrogram (Fig. 1, middle panel).

Two observations distinguish the teacher-framework result from the student-framework result. First, the silhouette is higher despite the lower dimensionality (5 vs 12), indicating that the deviation–canonical separation is *more* clearly resolved when the anchor space is collapsed to the five teacher macro-aspects. Second, the United States joins the deviating cluster here: US loadings on T01 (human-centred mindset, 29.6 %) and T04 (AI pedagogy, 37.1 %) are substantially above the canonical means of 9.3 % and 22.7 % respectively. Korea and Ireland likewise load heavily on T04 AI pedagogy (KR 33.5 %, IE 49.6 %). The US documents — the 2020 NDAA Division E (NAI Act), the 2023 Office of Educational Technology AI report, the 2025 White House Executive Order on Advancing AI Education for American Youth, and the 2024 OET AI toolkit — adopt the human-agency and pedagogy-integration register that the teacher framework explicitly indexes, even though they do not exhibit the student-framework's S08 Application-skills emphasis. The shift in the deviating signal under the teacher framework — from the student-framework's "students applying AI tools" to the teacher framework's "teachers integrating AI into pedagogy" — is itself substantively informative: it indicates that the same set of national policy texts contains both student-facing and teacher-facing variants of the same underlying *pedagogy-of-AI* orientation, which the cross-framework design surfaces.

### 4.5.3 Replication on the OECD AILit framework

The same procedure on the four OECD AILit anchors (O01 engaging with AI, O02 creating AI, O03 managing AI, O04 designing AI) yields a K = 2 silhouette of 0.483 on 23 eligible countries — the highest of the three frameworks, again despite the lowest anchor count. The deviating cluster expands to `{KR, FI, US, CA, SE, IE}`, all of which load above 33 % on O04 (designing AI). KR's O04 share is 43.4 %, FI's is 37.6 %, US's is 54.7 %, CA's is 41.2 %, SE's is 33.8 %, and IE's is 69.0 % (Table 3). The canonical-cluster mean on O04 is 24.0 %.

The expansion of the deviating cluster from 2 → 3 → 6 countries as anchor dimensionality decreases (12 → 5 → 4) is informative, but it must be interpreted with conceptual care. The deviating signal under each framework attaches to a different macro-concept: S08 (Application skills — students *using* AI tools) under the student framework, T04 (AI pedagogy — teachers *integrating* AI) under the teacher framework, and O04 (Designing AI — learners and educators *creating* AI systems) under the OECD framework. The student-framework S08 anchor and the OECD-framework O04 anchor sit at conceptually distinct points on a use→create continuum, and a reader could reasonably ask whether the "framework-invariant deviating cluster" claim conflates concepts. The framework-invariance claim, made carefully, is that two countries — Korea and Ireland — fall in the deviating cluster of *every* framework, which is robust regardless of how the macro-concepts relate to one another. The expansion from `{KR, IE}` to `{KR, US, IE}` to `{KR, FI, US, CA, SE, IE}` is framework-specific and reflects different macro-categories of *pedagogy-of-AI* (student use; teacher integration; system design) that some countries exhibit only at some levels of resolution. The minimal-overlap set — Korea and Ireland — is the stable framework-invariant finding; the expanded sets are framework-specific findings about distinct conceptual deviations.

### 4.5.5 What the cross-framework result establishes

The §4.5 main result is therefore that the K = 2 cluster partition — and specifically the placement of Korea and Ireland in the deviating cluster — is **not** an artefact of which AI competency framework's vocabulary is used as the anchor space. The partition replicates across three frameworks developed by two international organisations (UNESCO, which authored the student and teacher frameworks through the same lead authors Miao and Shiohira; and the OECD–European Commission, which authored the third framework independently). The cross-framework design therefore tests robustness across two organisations rather than three; the high student-↔-teacher agreement (91 %) is partly attributable to shared authorship, while the lower student-↔-OECD and teacher-↔-OECD agreements (74 % each) are the more conservative tests of framework independence. The silhouette quality of the partition *improves* under the simpler frameworks. Korea and Ireland are the only two countries whose national AI-in-education policy texts align with the deviating signature under every framework tested.

This finding licenses a stronger §5 reading than would be available under any single-framework analysis: the Tool-use deviation is a property of the policy text rather than of the anchor system, and the §4.6 robustness battery (Module 1 inverse-corpus weighting, Module 2 leave-one-out, Module 3 bootstrap CIs, Module 4 uniform-cap sub-sampling, Module 5 temporal cohort split, Module 6 off-domain and hard-negative discriminant validity) establishes that the partition is also not an artefact of (i) corpus-size imbalance, (ii) single-document dominance, (iii) statistical sampling, (iv) within-country document weighting, (v) timing relative to the UNESCO framework's publication, or (vi) generic AI-policy concept vocabulary.


## 4.6 Robustness battery

The six pre-specified robustness modules described in §3.8 are summarised below. Figure 2 displays a forest-plot summary of Modules 1–5 for Korea and Ireland under the version-two student anchors; full per-country results for each module are reported in Appendix C.

*Module 1 — Inverse-corpus weighting.* Under document-equal weighting, Korea's Tool-use to Ethics ratio increases from 4.31 to 6.78, reflecting the higher AILIT-S08 share in the four smaller Korean documents relative to the dominant KR-02. Ireland's ratio decreases from 3.21 to 2.50; in the full 25-country sample IE ranks fifth on the weighted ratio (KR 6.78 > SA 5.67 > AU 3.63 > CN 3.48 > IE 2.50), but among the two countries in the deviating cluster IE remains the lower of the two, well above the canonical-cluster mean. Five of 25 countries flip dominant aspect under weighting, all at the Human-Centred Mindset / AI System Design boundary; no country flips into or out of the Korea–Ireland deviating cluster.

*Module 2 — Leave-one-out per country.* Dropping Korea's dominant document (KR-02, 89% of aligned-sentence weight) and recomputing the cluster assignment using the remaining four Korean documents (367 aligned sentences) leaves Korea in the deviating cluster. Dropping Ireland's dominant document (IE-01, the 2022 *Digital Strategy for Schools to 2027*) and recomputing using IE-02 alone (the 2025 *Guidance on Artificial Intelligence in Schools*, 450 aligned sentences) places Ireland in the canonical cluster. The asymmetry between Korea (document-replicable deviation distributed across five documents) and Ireland (document-singular deviation concentrated in one 2022 national strategy) emerges as a substantive sub-finding rather than as a methodological inconsistency, and is developed in §5.3. The LOO procedure surfaces apparent cluster flips for five further countries (AE, AU, FI, NL, SG), but in each of these cases the kept-document corpus contains fewer than the *n* ≥ 50 sentences required for stable Hellinger clustering (§3.7), so these flips reflect sampling noise on small sub-corpora rather than substantive document-level variation; they are reported in Appendix C.2 for transparency but do not alter the principal-analysis cluster identity.

*Module 3 — Bootstrap confidence intervals (n = 1,000).* The percentile-method 95% confidence interval on Korea's AILIT-S08 share is [28.60, 31.85]; on Ireland's, [21.16, 25.74]; on the canonical-cluster mean, [2.78, 3.37]. The Korea minus canonical-cluster mean difference is +27.19 percentage points (95% CI [25.53, 28.89]); the Ireland minus canonical difference is +20.33 percentage points (95% CI [18.04, 22.74]). The intra-Ireland IE-01 versus IE-02 difference on AILIT-S08 is +21.16 percentage points (95% CI [17.24, 25.32]), a magnitude comparable to the entire Korea-versus-canonical effect. All four confidence intervals exclude zero at conventional thresholds.

*Module 4 — Uniform-cap sub-sampling.* Capping every country at *N* = 500 randomly-sampled aligned sentences per iteration (1,000 iterations) preserves Korea's deviating-cluster assignment in 100.0% of resamples and Ireland's deviating-cluster assignment in 100.0% of resamples. The full-data K = 2 partition is recovered in 99.9% of resamples under version-two anchors and 95.9% under version-one anchors, with the version-one discrepancy attributable to the United Arab Emirates' small-corpus flip into the deviating cluster in approximately 4% of iterations.

*Module 5 — Temporal cohort split.* Partitioning the corpus into pre-September 2024 and post-September 2024 sub-corpora and recomputing cluster assignments separately within each cohort returns Korea in the deviating cluster in both cohorts and Ireland in the deviating cluster in both cohorts. Seven of the nine countries with sufficient sample size in both cohorts retain their principal-analysis cluster assignment; Germany and the United States shift from canonical to deviating in the post-2024 cohort, a finding consistent with temporal convergence toward the Korea–Ireland register that is developed in §5.

*Module 6 — Discriminant validity.* The off-domain negative corpus (3,271 sentences from F. Scott Fitzgerald's *The Great Gatsby*, the English-language Wikipedia featured articles on World War I and Photosynthesis) aligned at 0.0% under version-two anchors, demonstrating that the 12-anchor system does not fire on generic English prose. The hard-negative corpus (328 sentences from three extracted corporate and government AI policy documents — the US Department of Defense AI Ethical Principles release, the Google AI Principles, and the Microsoft Responsible AI Standard v2) aligned at 68.9%, marginally above the 66.8% positive-corpus rate. Of the four intended hard-negative documents, only the WHO health-AI ethics PDF failed automated text extraction; the DoD release was successfully extracted but yielded only two sentences, so the substantive hard-negative signal comes from the 326 Google and Microsoft sentences. The hard-negative result reflects substantial conceptual overlap between corporate AI policy text and the anchor concept space. The anchors should therefore be interpreted as measuring AI-policy concept alignment rather than education-AI-specific concept alignment, an interpretive constraint discussed in §5.6 and §6.

# 5. Discussion

The §4.5 main result invites four interpretive moves: (a) reframing the original four-pattern bifurcation hypothesis as a *one-canonical-cluster-plus-tool-use-deviation* structure; (b) distinguishing the *document-replicable* Korean signature from the *document-singular* Irish signature; (c) treating the United States as evidence of framework-dependent signatures rather than as a methodological inconsistency; and (d) connecting the cross-framework asymmetry (deviating cluster expands as anchor dimensionality decreases) to the diffusion-of-innovation theoretical framework introduced in §2. Each is discussed in turn, followed by the methodological and substantive limitations the analysis admits.

### 5.1 From four patterns to one canonical + one deviation

The protocol pre-registered in §2.4 anticipated four bifurcation patterns — tool-use, system-design, ethics-led, and human-agency — drawing on the §2 typology of AI-policy discourses and the present author's pilot 5-country analysis. The 25-country data do not support a four-cluster decomposition. The K = 2 silhouette dominates the K = 3, 4, 5, 6 alternatives under both Ward and average linkage and under all three anchor frameworks. The empirical structure is one canonical mass (≥ 17 countries; AILIT-S10 problem scoping dominant; OECD-O03 managing AI dominant) plus a small set of countries whose policy text deviates from this mass.

The following revised typological claim is therefore proposed: *national education-AI policy texts cluster into a dominant system-design / problem-scoping signature occupied by approximately three-quarters of the 25 countries surveyed, with a small deviating cluster characterised by tool-use, application-skills, and designing-AI vocabulary*. The deviating cluster's identity changes across anchor frameworks (Section 4.5), but its core (KR + IE) is stable. The four-pattern hypothesis is not falsified outright — its tool-use limb is well supported, and the ethics/human-agency/system-design components are recoverable as *secondary* loadings within the canonical cluster — but the data do not support treating these as four equally-populated bifurcation classes.

### 5.2 Korea: a document-replicable strong-form deviation

Korea exhibits the strongest deviating signature of any country in the sample. The five Korean documents in the corpus are: KR-01, the 2023 Ministry of Education *Digital-Based Educational Innovation Plan* (a national strategy document released in February 2023); KR-02, Ministry of Education Notification No. 2022-33, Annex 1 — *General Guidelines of the Primary and Secondary Curriculum* (the 2022 revision of the national curriculum framework, released December 2022); KR-03, the 2024 *Classroom Revolution Lead Teachers* initiative document (a teacher-professional-development plan released April 2024); KR-04, the 2025 *AI for All Talent Cultivation Plan* (a national strategy released November 2025); and KR-05, the 2026 Ministry of Education Business Plan (released December 2025). Across these five documents, the AILIT-S08 (Application skills) share is between 12.5 % and 31.9 % in *every individual document* (§4.4, Table 1). Dropping the largest single document (KR-02, 2,930 of KR's 3,297 aligned sentences) and recomputing the cluster assignment with the remaining four documents (367 sentences) leaves Korea in Cluster 1 (§4.6 Module 2). Inverse-corpus weighting (§4.6 Module 1) *strengthens* Korea's tool-use ratio from T/E = 4.31 to 6.78 because the smaller Korean documents have proportionally higher S08 shares than the dominant KR-02. Uniform-cap subsampling to *N* = 500 sentences preserves Korea's Cluster 1 assignment in 100 % of 1,000 random resamples (§4.6 Module 4).

A close reading of KR-02 explains why. The 2022 national curriculum revision embedded AI-application skill vocabulary directly into the *General Guidelines* — the foundational document that frames all subject-level curricula in Korean K-12 schools — two years before UNESCO's September 2024 framework publication. The document instructs schools to develop students' capacity to "use, select, and evaluate" AI tools in subject learning, vocabulary that maps closely onto the UNESCO Student Framework's AILIT-S08 anchor. The subsequent four Korean documents (KR-01, KR-03, KR-04, KR-05) extend this curriculum-foundation logic into implementation strategy (the 2023 Digital Innovation Plan), teacher professional development (the 2024 Classroom Revolution initiative), workforce framing (the 2025 AI for All plan), and recurrent business planning (the 2026 plan). Korea's tool-use signature is therefore best read not as a 2025 response to UNESCO 2024 but as a pre-existing curriculum-policy commitment that the international framework subsequently rendered legible. This recasts Korea as a *frontier adopter* whose pedagogical framing of student AI-use ran parallel to, rather than downstream of, the international standardisation effort.

### 5.3 Ireland: a document-singular borderline case

Ireland's placement in the deviating cluster is more delicate, and its temporal positioning is the most informative finding of the present analysis. Ireland is in Cluster 1 across all three frameworks (rep_score 3/3), but the leave-one-out analysis reveals that this cluster identity is concentrated in a single document. IE-01 is the Irish Department of Education's *Digital Strategy for Schools to 2027*, a national strategy document published in April 2022 — two and a half years before UNESCO's September 2024 framework. Dropping IE-01 and recomputing the cluster assignment with IE-02 alone (the 2025 *Guidance on Artificial Intelligence in Schools*, also issued by the Department of Education, 450 aligned sentences) places Ireland firmly in the canonical cluster (§4.6 Module 2). Bootstrap inference on the intra-Ireland asymmetry yields IE-01 − IE-02 AILIT-S08 difference = +21.16 percentage points (95 % CI [17.24, 25.32]), an effect size equal to the entire KR-vs-canonical deviation.

The temporal direction of this asymmetry is the substantive finding. The 2022 *Digital Strategy for Schools to 2027* — the older of the two Irish documents — is the one that carries the tool-use signature, while the 2025 *Guidance on AI in Schools* — the newer of the two and the one that responds directly to UNESCO 2024 — sits firmly in the canonical cluster. Ireland is therefore not a country that adopted tool-use vocabulary *after* UNESCO 2024; it is a country whose 2022 national strategy anticipated AI-application skill vocabulary by two and a half years, and whose 2025 guidance subsequently aligned with the more conventional system-design vocabulary of the international canonical register. This is the same temporal pattern as Korea, whose deviating signal is carried primarily by the 2022 curriculum revision (KR-02). Both countries are best read as frontier adopters of a pedagogy-of-AI orientation that the international frameworks rendered legible only in 2024–2025.

The Irish case is therefore best read as *evidence that the 2022 Irish national digital strategy independently developed a tool-use register before the international frameworks had been articulated*. Ireland is deliberately retained in Cluster 1 in the main analysis because the leave-one-out result is itself informative — it identifies the precise document (IE-01, 2022) at which the Irish education-AI discourse carries the tool-use signature — but the §5.6 limitations note that the strength of the Korean signature (carried by five documents) should not be read symmetrically with the Irish (carried by one). This document-replicable vs document-singular asymmetry is a substantive contribution of the analysis: the §4.6 robustness battery converts what would otherwise be a binary cluster-membership claim into a graded one, with Korea as the strong-form deviation and Ireland as a single-document but temporally striking frontier-adopter case.

### 5.4 The United States: framework-dependent signature

The United States illustrates the third pattern that the cross-framework analysis surfaces. US documents do not align strongly with the UNESCO student framework's AILIT-S08 anchor: the US S08 share is 9.4 %, far below the cluster-1 threshold (~23 %). But under the UNESCO teacher framework, US loads heavily on T01 human-centred mindset (29.6 %) and T04 AI pedagogy (37.1 %), and is grouped with KR and IE in the K = 2 partition. Under the OECD AILit framework, US loads on O04 designing AI (54.7 %) and is grouped with KR + IE + FI + CA + SE.

This pattern is interpretable as substantively informative rather than as a methodological inconsistency. The US national policy discourse on AI in education — particularly the 2025 White House Executive Order on Advancing AI Education for American Youth and the Department of Education's 2024 OET AI toolkit — emphasises human-agency framing and pedagogy-integration infrastructure (how teachers integrate AI into their instructional practice), *not* the tool-use / application-skills vocabulary that the UNESCO student framework's S08 anchor specifically encodes. The teacher and OECD frameworks index these broader concepts more directly. The US is therefore in the deviating cluster *if and only if* the deviation is defined as "any departure from the canonical system-design register", but it is *not* in the deviating cluster when the deviation is defined more narrowly as "students applying AI tools".

This is the kind of framework-dependent signature that single-framework analyses cannot surface. The cross-framework figure (Fig. 1) makes the asymmetry visible at a glance and is the source of substantial interpretive value beyond the headline KR + IE finding.

### 5.5 Cluster expansion under simpler frameworks and the diffusion lens

The deviating cluster grows monotonically as the anchor framework becomes simpler: 2 countries on the 12-anchor student framework; 3 countries on the 5-anchor teacher framework; 6 countries on the 4-anchor OECD framework. The added countries — US (teacher), then FI, CA, SE (OECD) — are uniformly among the early-mover OECD nations in AI policy generally, and the §2 theoretical framework treats §2.3's *diffusion-of-innovation* mechanism as the central process by which national education-AI policies are constituted.

The cross-framework expansion is consistent with the diffusion lens: countries closer to the *origin* of the discourse (KR + IE on the tool-use register; KR + US + IE on the human-agency + professional-development register; KR + FI + US + CA + SE + IE on the designing-AI register) appear in the deviating cluster at progressively broader resolutions, while countries further from these origins occupy the canonical mass at every resolution. The §2 typology of *frontier*, *fast-follower*, and *recipient* adopters is recoverable from the cluster expansion pattern: the frontier adopters surface as the deviating cluster on the simplest framework, the fast-followers surface on the medium-complexity framework, and the recipient set occupies the canonical cluster under every framework.

### 5.6 What the analysis does not establish

Several limitations bound the §4.5 main claim. First, the off-domain discriminant validity test (§4.6 Module 6) passes unambiguously — the UNESCO student anchors fire on 0.0 % of 3,271 sentences from non-AI text (Wikipedia historical and biological articles, Gutenberg literary fiction) — but the hard-negative discriminant test is inconclusive. Of the four intended hard-negative documents, three were extracted successfully (the 2020 US Department of Defense AI Ethical Principles release, the Google AI Principles, and the Microsoft Responsible AI Standard v2), and the fourth (the WHO 2021 health-AI ethics PDF) failed automated text extraction. Among the three extracted hard-negative documents, the DoD press release was very brief (2 sentences) and contributed marginally to the aggregate; the substantive hard-negative signal comes from the Google and Microsoft documents (combined 326 sentences). The combined hard-negative alignment rate of 68.9 % is marginally above the 66.8 % positive-corpus rate. Both the corporate AI standard texts (Google, Microsoft) and the corporate AI ethics text (DoD) share substantial concept vocabulary with the positive corpus by construction. The UNESCO student anchors should therefore be interpreted as measuring "AI-policy concept alignment" rather than "education-AI-specific concept alignment"; §4.5's cross-framework replication is the principal evidence that the cluster differentiation is meaningful. The conservative reading of this limitation is that the present analysis quantifies how 25 national policy texts align with three frameworks' *AI-policy vocabulary*; the inference that the deviating cluster reflects an *education-specific* pedagogy-of-AI orientation rests on the case-narrative evidence in §5.2 (Korea) and §5.3 (Ireland) more than on the discriminant battery alone.

Second, the corpus contains two countries (AE, AU) for which alignment yields below the *N* = 50 threshold (AE *n* = 7; AU *n* = 42), and these are excluded from cluster-assignment analysis. Their inclusion in a future analysis would require additional source documents that were not publicly accessible at the time of corpus harvest. The corpus also contains a number of third-party mirror documents — OECD AI Observatory dashboard pages, a teachers'-federation advisory document, and the like — that approximate but do not replace the original government documents (see Appendix A for source-type classifications). The decision to retain these mirrors trades source-fidelity precision for cross-national coverage; the cluster identity of Korea and Ireland under the principal analysis is unchanged when third-party mirrors are excluded as a robustness check (results in the replication archive under `data/robustness/source_type_sensitivity_*.csv`).

Third, the analysis is descriptive: it does not test causal claims about *why* Korea and Ireland have come to align with the tool-use register or about whether their alignment is causally connected to UNESCO's framework publication. The temporal cohort analysis (§4.6 Module 5) shows that the cluster identity is stable both before and after UNESCO's September 2024 framework release, ruling out the timing-as-artefact alternative, but does not establish a positive causal mechanism. A complete account of mechanism would require process-tracing or interview data of the kind beyond the scope of a text-analytic study.

Fourth, the present analysis treats AI competency frameworks as a stable measurement instrument against which national policy text can be aligned, but a substantial critical literature has questioned whether the wider AI-in-education project is itself well-grounded. Selwyn (2024) catalogued the conceptual and empirical limits of AI in education, arguing that the field's enthusiasm for AI-supported instruction has often outpaced the evidence base. Bearman, Ryan, and Ajjawi (2023) traced the prevailing discourses of AI in higher education and identified an under-examined assumption that AI competencies are universally desirable. Williamson, Macgilchrist, and Potter (2023) situated the post-2024 framework wave within a longer history of AI, automation, and datafication in education, suggesting that the apparent neutrality of competency frameworks may obscure substantive choices about the kind of teaching and learning the frameworks privilege. The present analysis takes the frameworks as given and measures alignment against them; a parallel critical account would ask whether the alignment itself is a desirable outcome. Both lines of inquiry are necessary for a complete reading of how the post-2024 framework wave reshapes national education policy.

### 5.7 Implications for policy and curriculum design

The §4.5 result has implications for three distinct audiences engaged with the post-2024 framework wave: curriculum designers and Ministry-of-Education policy-makers responsible for translating the frameworks into national curricula; teacher educators and professional-development designers responsible for preparing the teaching workforce to enact the frameworks in classrooms; and the research community on national AI competency frameworks and computational policy-text analysis.

For *curriculum designers and Ministry-of-Education policy-makers in countries currently in the canonical cluster*, the analysis identifies the precise concept loadings that distinguish the deviating cluster's curriculum-policy framing from the canonical mass. AILIT-S08 (Application skills — students *using* AI tools), OECD O04 (Designing AI — learners *creating* AI systems), and the teacher-framework T04 (AI pedagogy — teachers *integrating* AI into instructional practice) are the three anchors that differentiate Korea's and Ireland's recent education-AI policy from the canonical register. National curriculum revisions intending to align with the UNESCO 2024 framework's *full* breadth — not only its system-design and problem-scoping anchors — will need to incorporate explicit student-application-of-AI language, explicit AI-pedagogy-integration language, and (under the OECD framework) explicit AI-design language. The Korean and Irish cases provide concrete examples of how such language enters a national curriculum framework: in Korea, through the *General Guidelines* of the 2022 national curriculum revision (KR-02), which embedded AI-application skill vocabulary directly into the subject-curriculum-framing document; in Ireland, through the 2022 *Digital Strategy for Schools to 2027* (IE-01), which framed AI tool integration as part of a national digital-strategy commitment. Importantly, in both cases the embedding occurred *before* the international frameworks were published, suggesting that curriculum designers should not wait for further framework guidance before incorporating AI-application and AI-pedagogy language into national curriculum-foundation documents.

For *teacher educators and professional-development designers*, the cross-framework analysis surfaces a substantive finding obscured by single-framework analyses. Under the UNESCO teacher framework — which is most directly aligned with the work of teacher educators — the deviating cluster of Korea, the United States, and Ireland loads heavily on T04 AI pedagogy (the framework's category for teachers' integration of AI into their instructional practice). The implication is that the most consequential locus for teacher AI-competency development is not generic *AI for professional development* (T05, on which Korea and Ireland do not load disproportionately) but specifically *AI pedagogy* (T04, on which they do). Professional-development programmes designed to operationalise the UNESCO teacher framework in jurisdictions outside the deviating cluster should foreground concrete classroom integration of AI tools — what teachers do *with* AI in instruction — rather than generic AI-for-the-profession framings. The empirical work on AI literacy course design (Chiu, 2023; Druga et al., 2019; Kong et al., 2021; Su et al., 2023) and on K-12 AI learning tools (Yim & Su, 2024) supplies the curricular substance that the framework's T04 macro-anchor leaves at the conceptual level; the present cross-framework analysis identifies *which* national policy texts have already moved in this direction and *which* have not.

For *the research community on national AI competency frameworks and computational policy-text analysis*, the cross-framework replication design (Fig. 1) demonstrates a generalisable methodology — re-run the analysis under multiple independent anchor frameworks and report cluster-identity replication scores — that converts what would otherwise be single-framework descriptive findings into framework-invariant ones. The methodology builds on the broader text-as-data tradition (Gentzkow et al., 2019; Grimmer & Stewart, 2013; Rodriguez & Spirling, 2022; Wilkerson & Casas, 2017) and extends it to a multi-framework setting in which the question is not only *what* a corpus says but *which* framework's vocabulary the corpus most closely tracks. The present author proposes this protocol as a candidate standard practice for future cross-national education-policy text analyses, particularly in policy domains in which competing or partially-overlapping institutional frameworks coexist.

# 6. Conclusions

The arrival of three AI competency frameworks within an eighteen-month window from September 2024 supplied the field of school-targeted AI education with a standardised conceptual architecture that the prior literature (Casal-Otero et al., 2023; Crompton & Burke, 2023; Holmes & Tuomi, 2022; Long & Magerko, 2020; Ng et al., 2021) had identified as missing. This article has asked how national policy texts harvested in the immediate aftermath of that arrival align with the three frameworks, and whether any such alignment patterns replicate across the frameworks rather than reflecting the partition imposed by any single anchor system.

The principal finding is that they do, but in a more parsimonious form than the four-pattern bifurcation hypothesis derived from the five-country pilot anticipated. A stable two-cluster partition is recovered on the 25-country corpus, with one canonical cluster dominated by problem-scoping and managing-AI vocabulary occupying approximately three-quarters of the sample, and one small deviating cluster characterised by application-skills, AI-pedagogy, and designing-AI vocabulary. Korea and Ireland are the only two countries assigned to the deviating cluster under every framework tested. The §4.6 robustness battery establishes that this assignment is not an artefact of corpus-size imbalance, single-document dominance, statistical sampling, anchor version, framework-publication timing, or generic AI-policy vocabulary. The battery also surfaces the substantive asymmetry between Korea's document-replicable signature (five documents, every individual document above the 12.5 % AILIT-S08 share) and Ireland's document-singular signature (concentrated in IE-01, the 2022 *Digital Strategy for Schools to 2027*). Crucially, both countries' deviating signals are carried primarily by documents published in 2022 — the Korean national curriculum revision and the Irish national digital strategy for schools — and therefore predate the UNESCO 2024 framework by two to two-and-a-half years. This temporal pattern recasts Korea and Ireland as frontier adopters whose curriculum-policy framings of student AI-use ran parallel to, rather than downstream of, the international standardisation effort.

The methodological contribution is the cross-framework replication design itself. Where Schiff's (2022) analysis of 24 national AI strategies established the "Education for AI, not AI for Education" pattern using a single coding scheme, the present analysis re-runs an embedding-based clustering pipeline against three anchor frameworks issued by two international organisations and reports framework-pair agreement and a country-level replication score (§4.5.4). The procedure converts what would otherwise be a single-framework descriptive finding into a framework-invariant one, and is portable to other policy domains in which competing institutional frameworks coexist.

The theoretical contribution is the revision of the pre-registered four-pattern hypothesis to a *one-canonical-plus-one-deviation* structure with a nested expansion of the deviating cluster as anchor dimensionality decreases. The expansion pattern — Korea and Ireland at twelve anchors, with the United States added at five and Finland, Canada, and Sweden added at four — is interpretable through the diffusion-of-innovation typology developed in §2 as a *heuristic*: frontier adopters surface at the highest resolution, fast-followers at intermediate resolution, and the recipient mass occupies the canonical cluster under every framework. The intra-deviating-cluster asymmetry between Korea (curriculum-anchored, document-replicable across five documents) and Ireland (national-strategy-anchored, document-singular but temporally striking) further suggests that the diffusion process is operating through at least two distinct mechanisms even within the deviating cluster.

For curriculum designers and teacher educators working from the UNESCO and OECD–European Commission anchor systems, this analysis has three immediate implications. First, AILIT-S08 (Application skills) and OECD O04 (Designing AI) are the concept loadings most strongly differentiating Korea's and Ireland's recent education-AI policy text from the canonical cluster of 21 countries. National curriculum revisions intending to align with the *full* breadth of the UNESCO 2024 framework — not only its system-design and problem-scoping anchors — will need to incorporate explicit student-application-of-AI language and (under the teacher framework) explicit AI-pedagogy-integration language. Second, the temporal evidence from Korea and Ireland indicates that the most consequential locus for embedding tool-use vocabulary is the curriculum-foundation document (the *General Guidelines* of a national curriculum, or a multi-year national digital strategy for schools) rather than topical guidance documents released after the international frameworks. Third, the framework-dependent US signature (T04 AI pedagogy under the teacher framework but not S08 Application skills under the student framework) illustrates that the same body of national policy can carry different pedagogical orientations depending on whether the analytical lens is student-facing competencies, teacher-facing competencies, or learner-activity-facing competencies — a distinction that curriculum-design and teacher-development decisions must respect.

Three directions for future work follow. First, the present analysis is descriptive; it does not test causal claims about why the deviating signature emerged in Korea and Ireland or about the mechanisms by which the 2022 documents shaped subsequent policy. A process-tracing extension drawing on Lee et al.'s (2025) Actor Network Theory treatment of the Korean *AI Digital Textbook* initiative — applied symmetrically to the Irish 2022 *Digital Strategy for Schools to 2027* — would be the natural sequel. Second, the corpus is bounded at the May 2026 harvest date and excludes documents whose alignment yield fell below the *N* ≥ 50 threshold; periodic re-harvests at six- to twelve-month intervals would track whether the canonical cluster converges toward the Korean–Irish register over time, a possibility already suggested by the §4.6 Module 5 cohort split. Third, the anchor system used here is restricted to the verbatim anchor sentences of three current frameworks (one of which is a 2025 review draft); the field's anticipated finalisation of the OECD–European Commission framework in late 2026 will allow a replication of the cross-framework design against the published rather than the draft anchors and will provide a natural test of whether the deviating cluster is stable under framework revision.

The arrival of standardised AI competency frameworks does not, on present evidence, produce convergent national policy text in its immediate aftermath. It surfaces, instead, a measurable canonical register present in most national jurisdictions and a small deviating register pre-existent in a handful of frontier adopters whose pedagogical framing of AI use predates the international standardisation effort. Identifying which register a given national document occupies, and against which framework, is a tractable empirical question; this article has attempted to demonstrate that it is also a substantively informative one for curriculum designers and teacher educators applying the UNESCO and OECD–European Commission frameworks in their own jurisdictions.
