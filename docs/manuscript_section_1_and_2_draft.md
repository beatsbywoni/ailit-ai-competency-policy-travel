# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 The arrival of three AI competency frameworks for school education

Within an eighteen-month window beginning in September 2024, the world's two principal multilateral education-policy organisations issued three AI competency frameworks for school education. UNESCO published the AI Competency Framework for Students (Miao & Shiohira, 2024a) and the AI Competency Framework for Teachers (Miao & Shiohira, 2024b) at its 2024 Digital Learning Week. The OECD and the European Commission jointly released the May 2025 review draft of *Empowering Learners for the Age of AI: An AI Literacy Framework for Primary and Secondary Education* (OECD & European Commission, 2025), with the final version expected by the close of 2026. Each framework offers a structured taxonomy of the knowledge, skills, attitudes, and ethical orientations that primary and secondary learners and their teachers are expected to develop in the era of generative and predictive AI. Each framework also functions, by intent of its publishers, as a benchmark against which national curricula and policies may be reviewed and revised.

These framework releases have coincided with an unusual concentration of national policy activity on AI in education. National-level documents — strategies, curricular guidance, teacher-development plans, and supplementary curricula — are being drafted, revised, and published across diverse jurisdictions at a pace that has few precedents in twenty-first-century education policy. The question this article addresses is how those national documents take up the conceptual architecture supplied by UNESCO and the OECD–European Commission: which framework concepts are foregrounded, which are absent, and whether national patterns of adherence resolve into a small number of identifiable adoption signatures.

## 1.2 The gap: cross-national measurement of framework adoption

Several constraints currently limit researchers' ability to answer that question. First, the primary documents are dispersed across national ministry portals in eight or more languages and are rarely indexed in standard policy databases. Second, the closest prior study of cross-national AI policy text analysed national AI strategies rather than education-specific policies and found that the instrumental value of education in supporting an AI-ready workforce dominated those documents, while AI in education itself was largely absent and the ethical implications of AI in educational settings received scant attention (Schiff, 2022). That study examined 24 English-language national AI strategy documents issued between 2016 and the beginning of 2020 using frame-based content analysis; it identified the "Education for AI, not AI for Education" pattern that has come to define the literature on this topic. The Schiff (2022) finding raises a follow-up question that is not yet addressed in the published literature: when policy documents are *explicitly* about education and artificial intelligence — that is, when the educational dimension is the subject of the document rather than an instrumental subsection of a broader AI strategy — does the same pattern hold, or does a different conceptual architecture emerge?

Third, even where such documents exist, the field of AI literacy itself is conceptually contested. Long and Magerko (2020) offered a foundational taxonomy of sixteen AI literacy competencies and design considerations. Ng et al. (2021) reviewed thirty peer-reviewed papers and proposed a four-aspect schema — know-and-understand, use-and-apply, evaluate-and-create, and ethical issues — that anticipates UNESCO's later four-aspect student framework with considerable structural overlap. Casal-Otero et al. (2023), in a systematic literature review of K–12 AI literacy interventions, documented a field characterised by methodological diversity and limited cross-study comparability. The arrival of UNESCO's and the OECD–European Commission's frameworks supplies, in principle, the standardised conceptual architecture that the field has lacked. Whether that architecture is reflected in national policy text remains an open empirical question.

## 1.3 Approach and research questions

This article addresses that question through a computational text analysis of 25 national AI-in-education policy texts harvested in May 2026. The analytical strategy aligns every corpus sentence against the verbatim anchor sentences of three independent AI competency frameworks — UNESCO student (twelve anchors), UNESCO teacher (five anchors), and OECD–European Commission (four anchors) — using multilingual sentence embeddings, and then clusters the resulting country-level adherence distributions using Hellinger distance. The cross-framework design is deliberate: it asks whether any cluster structure recovered from the principal UNESCO student analysis is preserved when the analysis is re-run against the other two frameworks, and thereby distinguishes framework-invariant adoption signatures from artefacts of any single anchor space.

Four research questions structure the analysis. RQ1: What cluster structure emerges from Hellinger-distance clustering of the 25-country adherence matrix against the UNESCO Student Framework's twelve anchors? RQ2: Does this cluster structure replicate when the analysis is re-run against the UNESCO teacher and OECD–European Commission frameworks? RQ3: Does the cluster identity of any country survive a six-module robustness battery comprising inverse-corpus weighting, leave-one-out, bootstrap confidence intervals, uniform-cap sub-sampling, temporal cohort split, and discriminant validity? RQ4: How does the resulting pattern of cluster identity map onto diffusion-of-innovation typologies of national policy adoption?

## 1.4 Contributions

This article makes three contributions to the literature on AI in education. First, it documents a stable K = 2 partition in which Korea and Ireland constitute a deviating cluster characterised by adherence to the UNESCO Student Framework's Application Skills anchor, while the remaining 21 sample countries occupy a canonical cluster anchored on Problem Scoping. The deviating cluster identity replicates across all three independent anchor frameworks tested, and the partition survives the six-module robustness battery, with the asymmetry between Korea (a document-replicable deviation) and Ireland (a document-singular deviation concentrated in one 2025 guidance document) emerging as a substantive sub-finding rather than as a methodological inconsistency.

Second, the cross-framework replication design supplies a generalisable methodological template for distinguishing framework-invariant from framework-specific findings in cross-national policy text analysis. The procedure — re-run the principal pipeline against each independent anchor framework, report framework-pair cluster-identity agreement, and compute a country-level replication score — is portable to other policy domains where competing institutional frameworks coexist.

Third, the analysis revises the pre-registered four-pattern bifurcation hypothesis that had been derived from a five-country pilot. The 25-country data do not support a four-cluster decomposition; instead, one canonical cluster dominates (with approximately three-quarters of the sample) and a small deviating cluster (Korea and Ireland in the principal analysis; expanding to {Korea, United States, Ireland} under the UNESCO teacher framework and to a six-country set under the OECD framework) departs from it. This nested expansion across frameworks of decreasing dimensionality is itself interpretable through the diffusion-of-innovation typology developed in §2.

The article proceeds as follows. Section 2 develops the theoretical framework. Section 3 details the corpus, anchors, alignment procedure, clustering, robustness battery, cross-framework replication, and reproducibility infrastructure. Section 4 reports the principal results and the robustness battery. Section 5 discusses the findings against the theoretical framework and identifies limitations. Section 6 concludes.

# 2. Theoretical framework

## 2.1 AI literacy as a structured construct

The construct of AI literacy has matured rapidly since Long and Magerko's (2020) foundational taxonomy of sixteen competencies — recognising the presence of AI, understanding its strengths and limits, communicating with AI, programming AI, and so on — was published in the proceedings of the 2020 CHI Conference. The Long and Magerko taxonomy treats AI literacy as a multidimensional construct combining cognitive, ethical, and practical competencies, structured by HCI-derived design considerations rather than by curricular sequence. Ng et al. (2021), reviewing thirty peer-reviewed studies, recast the construct into a four-aspect schema — know-and-understand, use-and-apply, evaluate-and-create, and ethical issues — that anticipates the four-aspect structure subsequently adopted by UNESCO's Student Framework: human-centred mindset, ethics of AI, AI techniques and applications, and AI system design. The structural convergence between Ng et al. (2021) and Miao and Shiohira (2024a) is striking and suggests that, at the macro-aspect level at which the present analysis operates, the underlying conceptual architecture of school-targeted AI literacy has reached a degree of definitional stability that the Casal-Otero et al. (2023) systematic review concluded was, in 2022, still emerging.

This convergence licenses the methodological choice central to the present analysis: treating the UNESCO and OECD–European Commission anchors as a stable measurement instrument rather than as one more contestable framework among many. Where measurement-instrument stability is uncontroversial, attention can shift from the construction of the anchors to the patterns of national adoption against them.

## 2.2 Three frameworks, three concept spaces

The three anchor frameworks differ in dimensionality and orientation. The UNESCO Student Framework's twelve anchors are organised in a four-aspect by three-progression-level matrix (understand, apply, create) and are pitched at the level of student-facing competencies (Miao & Shiohira, 2024a). The UNESCO Teacher Framework's five anchors collapse the four aspects into five teacher-facing macro-categories that include AI Pedagogy and AI for Professional Development, categories with no direct counterpart in the student framework (Miao & Shiohira, 2024b). The OECD–European Commission AI Literacy Framework reorganises the construct into four domains pitched at the level of learner activity rather than competency type: engaging with, creating, managing, and designing AI (OECD & European Commission, 2025). The three frameworks address overlapping conceptual territory but partition it differently, and their independent publication by two organisations provides an opportunity to test whether national policy adoption patterns are anchored to the territory itself or to the way any one framework partitions it.

## 2.3 Diffusion and isomorphism in cross-national policy adoption

National responses to international policy initiatives have been theorised under two complementary lenses. Rogers (2003), in the fifth edition of *Diffusion of Innovations*, distinguished innovators and early adopters from early majority, late majority, and laggards along a normal-curve adoption distribution and identified the social-system properties — communication channels, opinion leadership, and relative advantage — that shape diffusion speed. Applied to cross-national education-policy adoption, the Rogers typology suggests a three-tier reading of the sample: a small set of frontier adopters whose policy text predates or coincides with the framework publication and whose framing converges with the framework on substantive grounds, a larger middle tier of fast followers whose policy text post-dates the framework and adopts a portion of its vocabulary, and a residual set of recipients whose adoption is more nominal than substantive.

DiMaggio and Powell (1983) offered a complementary lens centred not on speed of adoption but on the *mechanism* of isomorphism — the process by which actors in an organisational field come to resemble one another. They distinguished coercive isomorphism (driven by external mandate), mimetic isomorphism (driven by uncertainty and the imitation of high-status models), and normative isomorphism (driven by professional socialisation). The post-2024 wave of national AI-in-education policy activity exhibits features consistent with all three mechanisms. UNESCO and OECD framework publication functions normatively; international ranking and benchmarking pressure functions coercively; and the absence of established national models in this rapidly emerging field encourages mimetic adoption of frontier exemplars. The empirical question this article addresses is whether any frontier exemplar is identifiable in the cross-national data.

## 2.4 The Korean case and the four-pattern hypothesis

Korea's *AI Digital Textbook* (AIDT) initiative, launched as a Ministry of Education flagship in 2023 and rolled out across selected subjects in 2025, has been the subject of recent critical scholarship as an exemplary case of EdTech policy assemblage in a frontier-adopter jurisdiction. Lee et al. (2025) traced the AIDT initiative's lifecycle from ideation through stakeholder mobilisation, institutional inscription, contestation, and partial reclassification, drawing on Actor Network Theory to characterise the policy as the unstable product of shifting human and non-human alignments. For present purposes the Lee et al. analysis matters as theoretical context: Korea has, over roughly the same period in which the international frameworks were being drafted, committed substantial state resources to a tool-use-oriented AI-in-education policy programme. If the diffusion and isomorphism lenses jointly apply, Korea ought to appear as a frontier adopter whose policy text exhibits a tool-use-oriented framing irrespective of which anchor framework is used.

A pre-registered hypothesis was developed during the pilot phase of the present study. Five pilot countries (Korea, the United Kingdom, Singapore, Finland, and the United States) suggested four possible adoption signatures: tool-use, system-design, ethics-led, and human-agency. The pilot interpretation hypothesised that these four signatures would resolve into four roughly equal clusters in the full 25-country analysis. The 25-country results reported in §4 do not support this four-cluster decomposition. The revision of the hypothesis — from a four-pattern bifurcation to a one-canonical-cluster-plus-one-deviating-cluster structure — is itself a finding of the analysis and is developed in §5.
